# Style guide: Mathematics 4MA1, Higher tier (Papers 1H and 2H)

This guide comes from reading the 32 Higher-tier papers, their mark schemes and the examiner reports in the private corpus. Everything here is my own description. Nothing is quoted beyond a few words.

## 1. Paper anatomy

- **Two papers, 1H and 2H.** They have the same format: 2 hours, 100 marks each, and a calculator is allowed in both. Each paper is worth 50% of the qualification. Neither paper has sections or optional questions.
- **Question count.** Each paper has 19 to 26 numbered questions. The difficulty ramps up through the paper. The first five or so questions are Foundation-overlap material worth 2 to 4 marks. The middle of the paper is standard Higher content. The last five or six questions are the hardest:
  - algebraic proof
  - harder quadratics and simultaneous equations
  - calculus applications
  - vector proofs
  - circle-theorem chains
  - probability problems that lead to a quadratic
- **Cover rubric.** The cover asks for black ink and says to answer every question in the spaces provided. It lists the materials needed: ruler, protractor, compasses, eraser and calculator, and it says tracing paper may be used. It warns that correct answers without enough working may earn no marks, and it tells candidates where the formulae page is. Advice lines say to read each question carefully, keep an eye on the time and check answers at the end.
- **Formulae page.** It sits at the front (`maths-formulae-higher`) and gives:
  - the arithmetic series sum
  - the quadratic formula
  - the trapezium area
  - the sine rule, cosine rule and $\frac12 ab\sin C$
  - the volumes of a prism, cylinder, cone and sphere
  - the curved surface areas of a cylinder, cone and sphere

  Candidates must remember everything else, including Pythagoras, circle area and arc length.
- **Page furniture.** Each question ends with a line showing its total marks. Each paper ends with a line showing the paper total. Answer lines are dotted and sit at the bottom right of the working space. They often carry a prefix such as `x = ...`, a unit (`cm²`, `km/h`) or a template such as `(...., ....)`. Graph questions provide a printed grid. Tables to complete are printed with blank cells.
- **Diagrams.** Most geometric figures carry a note in the bottom corner saying the diagram is not accurately drawn. There are two exceptions: grids and constructions, which are drawn accurately at true size.

## 2. Question types (qtypes) and tariffs

These are the corpus mark totals per question: 2 marks ×53, 3 ×222, 4 ×191, 5 ×168, 6 ×77, 7 ×30, 8 ×14, 9 ×3. The typical question is worth 3 to 5 marks.

| qtype | What it looks like | Tariff | Command words |
|---|---|---|---|
| `short` | A single routine skill: write a number as a product of primes, a standard-form calculation, a bearing, finding an angle and stating the theorem, simple vectors | 1–3 | Write down, Work out, Find, Calculate, Simplify |
| `problem` | Multi-step work in context: percentages, compound measures, bounds, mensuration in a real setting, without-replacement probability | 3–6 | Work out, Calculate, Show your working clearly |
| `algebra` | Manipulation: expand or factorise, solve, rearrange, algebraic fractions, completing the square, simultaneous equations | 2–5 | Solve, Factorise fully, Simplify fully, Make ... the subject, Expand and simplify |
| `proof` | "Show that" or "Prove" questions: algebraic proof with $2n$ / $2n+1$, recurring decimals, geometric reasoning with reasons, vector collinearity, showing a formula | 2–5 | Show that, Prove, Give a reason for each stage |
| `graph` | Plotting or reading on a printed grid: completing a table and drawing a curve, regions, transformations, constructions, cumulative frequency and histograms | 2–6 | Draw, Complete, On the grid, Use your graph to find |
| `stats` | Data handling: grouped mean, reading cumulative frequency or a histogram, comparing distributions, trees | 3–6 | Work out an estimate, Compare, Complete |
| `structured` | One context with (a), (b), (c)... parts that build on each other. Part tariffs are 1–3 and the total is 5–9 | 5–9 | mixed |

**Stem patterns.** Stems are short. A context or diagram comes first, then a one-line instruction. Accuracy is stated explicitly, for example "to 3 significant figures" or "to 1 decimal place". In my bank, the instruction says "Give your answer to 3 significant figures". "Show clear algebraic working" or "Show your working clearly" signals that a bare answer scores nothing. Typical contexts are tickets, savings, exchange rates, journeys, tanks and containers, spinners, bags of counters, plants and fish lengths, and survey data. Names are drawn from many cultures. No exam-board names appear in question text.

**How difficulty ramps.**

- Within a topic it moves from a direct calculation (1–3) to a reverse or inverse step (4–6) to a step that needs algebra or proof (7–9). For example:
  - percentage change, then reverse percentage, then compound interest solved for $n$
  - arc length, then arc length to angle to perimeter, then a segment area
- Across a paper, the final questions combine two topics. Examples include probability leading to a quadratic, circle theorems with algebra, and vectors with ratio.

## 3. How the mark scheme is written

- **Layout.** Each part has a table with columns *Answer*, *Marks* and *Notes*. Every scoring step has its own row. Marks are tagged:
  - **M** (method): awarded for a correct method, even with numerical slips
  - **A** (accuracy): depends on the preceding M mark
  - **B** (unconditional): for a correct value or statement
- **Abbreviations:** cao (correct answer only), ft (follow through), isw (ignore subsequent working), oe (or equivalent), SC (special case), dep (dependent), indep (independent), awrt (answers which round to), eeoo (each error or omission).
- **General marking principles.**
  - A correct answer with no working gets full marks only when no method is asked for and the answer could not have come from wrong working.
  - Where working is required, an answer alone scores 0.
  - If different methods are seen, the examiner marks the one leading to the final answer.
- **Accuracy.** The scheme gives the exact value plus an awrt value. Readings from graphs get a tolerance range, for example "60.5 to 68.5". Measured angles in constructions get ±2°.
- **Alternative methods.** These are set out as separate blocks or in the Notes column. Examples are the sine rule instead of tangents in elevation problems, or the area method instead of a scale reading in histograms.
- **B2 / B1 patterns.** A 2-mark B question awards B1 for a partially correct answer, for example "B1 for one correct coordinate", "B1 for 2 correct bars" or "B1 for two correct inequalities".
- **Reasons.** In geometry the reason marks are B marks. They depend on a correct method and need the full theorem wording. Abbreviations such as "alt angles" or "Z angles" are not accepted.
- **Show that.** The final A mark requires the given result to be reached with no incorrect working, and it depends on all the method marks.
- **AO split.**
  - AO1: number, algebra and functions
  - AO2: geometry and vectors
  - AO3: statistics and problem solving

  The bank tags each question with its main AO.

## 4. What the examiner reports say

Strong answers set out each method step, keep unrounded values until the end and state the theorem in full. Common weaknesses the reports return to:

- **Reverse percentages:** multiplying by 1.15 instead of dividing by 1.15.
- **Surds:** wrong expansion of $(a+\sqrt b)^2$. Candidates also forget to multiply the numerator by the conjugate when rationalising.
- **Completing the square with a leading coefficient other than 1,** especially a negative one.
- **Differentiation:** sign errors with negative powers. Candidates also forget to set $\frac{dy}{dx}=0$ for turning points, or use $s$ where $v$ is needed in kinematics.
- **Sectors:** mixing up arc length and sector area, and forgetting the two radii in a perimeter.
- **Histograms:** using frequency instead of frequency density, or ignoring the area when estimating part of a class.
- **Graph transformations:** moving $f(x+a)$ the wrong way, or confusing $-f(x)$ with $f(-x)$.
- **Algebraic fractions:** cancelling terms instead of factors.
- **Inverse and rearranging:** when the subject appears twice, candidates fail to factorise to isolate it.
- **Bounds:** using the wrong combination of upper and lower bounds in a division.
- **Transformations:** describing two transformations instead of one, or leaving out the centre or direction of a rotation.
- **Constructions:** construction arcs removed or never drawn.
- **Proof:** using numerical examples instead of a general algebraic argument, and giving no concluding statement.

The bank's mark-scheme notes target these errors, for example "radius must be halved" and "a combination of transformations scores B0".

**Forms.**

- Number and algebra appear most often as stand-alone short or algebra items.
- Geometry appears as diagram-led problems.
- Statistics appears as table- or graph-led multi-part questions.
- Calculus and vector proof sit almost always in the last third of a paper.
