# Style guide: Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English Literature 4ET1, Paper 1

Scope: Paper 1 (Poetry and Modern Prose) only. Section C is limited to *Of Mice and Men*. This guide is based on the private corpus: the specification (Issue 3), the sample assessment materials, Paper 1 question papers and mark schemes from June 2018 to June 2025 (including the 01R variants), examiners' reports and exemplar booklets. Everything below is described in our own words. Nothing longer than a few words is quoted.

## 1. Paper anatomy

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Code | 4ET1/01, with a paired 4ET1/01R paper (a different timezone version with different poems and questions) |
| Time | 2 hours |
| Marks | 90 (60% of the qualification) |
| Rubric | Answer THREE questions: the Section A question, ONE question from Section B and ONE question from Section C |
| Section A | Unseen poetry. One compulsory question, 20 marks, AO2 only. Suggested time 35 minutes |
| Section B | Anthology poetry. Choose ONE of two comparison questions, 30 marks, AO2 15 + AO3 15. Suggested time 40 minutes |
| Section C | Modern prose. Two questions (EITHER/OR) on each set text; answer one. 40 marks, AO1 20 + AO4 20. Suggested time 45 minutes |
| Booklets | The question booklet has a contents list showing page numbers for each section and each novel. Answers go in a separate answer booklet, where the candidate crosses the box for the question chosen. A clean copy of the anthology poems is supplied. The paper is closed-book for the novel |
| Furniture | "Turn over" and the paper reference on every page. Each question ends with a "(Total for Question n = X marks)" line, and each section ends with a section total |

The Covid-period series (June 2021, November 2021, January 2022, June 2022) used a shortened 1 hour 20 minute paper with two sections only: unseen poetry plus modern prose. The full three-section structure returned from November 2022 onwards.

## 2. Question types

### Section A: unseen poem (20 marks)
* The paper says "Read the following poem." Then it prints a modern, accessible poem of about 12 to 30 lines, with line numbers every five lines, the poet's name and an approximate date, and asterisked glosses for unusual words.
* The stem is always the same formula: *Explore how the writer presents [focus] in this poem.* Three bullet prompts follow (descriptive skills / choice of language / use of form and structure). The question ends by asking for support with examples from the poem.
* Focuses seen in the corpus are concrete and accessible. Examples: a family; concerns for the planet; turning ten; a parent's thoughts about a child; strong feelings; a cottage and the people in it; a power cut; neighbours; nature; a child's wishes; a journey home; a memory of an event; gathering leaves; two brothers. The difficulty comes from the poem's subtlety, not from the wording of the question.
* Poems are mostly 20th-century or contemporary and are often about childhood and family. Our bank adds some pre-1929 public-domain poems, which are harder (7-9 band) because of their older vocabulary.

### Section B: anthology comparison (30 marks)
* **Q2 (two named poems):** the candidate is told to re-read two named poems, then compare how the writers present a theme in them, with a reminder to refer to language, form and structure and to support the answer from the poems.
* **Q3 (one named poem plus one of the candidate's choice):** the candidate is told to re-read one named poem, then compare how a theme is presented in it and in one other poem from the anthology.
* Themes used: identity and language; feelings about love; the passing of time; giving advice; sadness; power; isolation; recollections of the past; a strong point of view; family relationships; emotions; vivid description; being alone; the story of an event.
* Our bank keeps this two-question structure. We changed the rubric wording slightly ("Look again at ... in the collection", "Compare how the poets present ...") so that the bank never copies the board's exact sentences.

### Section C: modern prose essay (40 marks)
* Each novel has two questions. Usually one is about a character or relationship and the other is about a theme or the writer's methods. Every question ends with the instruction that the candidate must consider the context of the novel.
* Stem patterns: *Explore the character of ...*, *In what ways is X significant/important ...*, *Discuss the role of ...*, *Examine the theme of ...*, *Which one character do you consider to be the most ...*. Some questions add a short epigraph, either a quotation from the novel or a critical statement, followed by *How far do you agree with this view?*
* **There are no bullet-point prompts in Section C** on any real Paper 1 from 2018 to 2025. Bullet prompts appear only under the Section A question. Our bank follows the real paper.
* We cannot quote the novel, so our epigraph-style questions use invented critical statements, with no fake attribution.

## 3. Mark schemes
* Every Paper 1 question is levels-marked. There are no point-marked rows. Each grid has five levels plus a 0 band ("no rewardable material"), and examiners mark on a best fit within each level.
  * Section A: 1-4 / 5-8 / 9-12 / 13-16 / 17-20. Descriptors run from simple identification, through mostly descriptive comment, understanding of a range of features linked to effect, and focused, sustained analysis, up to a cohesive evaluation of how language, form and structure work together, with discriminating choice of evidence.
  * Section B: 1-6 / 7-12 / 13-18 / 19-24 / 25-30. Each level adds a comparison strand, from little or no comparison up to perceptive comparison across a comprehensive range. **An answer that covers only one poem cannot go higher than the top of Level 2.**
  * Section C: 1-8 / 9-16 / 17-24 / 25-32 / 33-40. Descriptors run from limited, through some, sound and thorough, to assured knowledge and personal engagement, with context ranging from little comment to being convincingly integrated.
* Indicative content follows each question. In Section A it is grouped under the three bullet prompts. In Section B it is grouped by AO2 for each poem, then AO3 comparison points. In Section C it is grouped into AO1 bullets, then AO4 bullets. Every set ends with a reminder that these are suggestions only and that any valid response is credited.
* General guidance says marking is positive, the full range of marks is used, and summary or simple feature-spotting is not enough.

## 4. What the examiners' reports say
* Stronger answers explore the deeper meaning of a poem, analyse language, form and structure together instead of in separate paragraphs, and comment on the effect on the reader. In Section B they compare throughout, not just in a final paragraph.
* Stronger prose answers use short embedded quotations or precise references, answer the question directly, and weave context into the argument.
* Weaker answers retell the story, quote at length, list techniques without effect, or bolt context on at the start or end. Some write about only one poem in Section B, which caps the answer at Level 2.
* *Of Mice and Men* is by far the most popular prose text. Responses are usually split fairly evenly between its two questions.

## 6. How the bank reproduces this
* qtypes: `unseen-poem` (20), `anthology-pair` (30), `anthology-open` (30), `prose-character` (40), `prose-theme` (40). The full-paper preset is Q1 / Q2 or Q3 / Q6 or Q7.
* Unseen poems are either originals written for the bank or genuinely public-domain poems, with poet, dates and provenance recorded. Anthology poems are never printed. *Of Mice and Men* is never quoted.
