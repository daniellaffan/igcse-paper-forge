Parts (a) to (c) are based on Text One, *After the Water*, by Tomás Reyes, in the Insert. Part (d) is based on Text Two, the extract from *A Passage to Africa*, from Part 1 of the Anthology (use your own copy). Part (e) is based on both texts.
Text One: After the Water
   The boat smelt of diesel and wet rope. For three hours we nosed between the tops of
   trees, the engine coughing whenever the propeller caught on something hidden beneath
   the brown water. Somewhere under us, the boatman told me, was the road we would
   normally have driven along. I tried to imagine it: the tarmac, the lorries, the
 5 stalls selling roasted corn. All I could see was water, flat and sullen, stretching
   to a sky that looked as though it had been scrubbed with ash.

   I had covered floods before. I knew what to expect, or thought I did. In my bag were
   three cameras, eleven lenses and a notebook already half full of figures: the number
   of villages cut off, the number of families displaced, the tonnes of grain lost.
10 Figures are what editors ask for first. They fit neatly into a headline. They do not
   smell, or cry, or ask you for help.

   We reached Kanta just after midday. It had been a village of perhaps four hundred
   people. Now it was an island of rooftops and one concrete school building, its upper
   floor crowded with everyone who had nowhere else to go. Washing hung from the
15 windows. A goat stood on a corrugated roof, bleating at the water as if it could
   argue it away. On the steps that led down into the flood, a line of women were
   rinsing pots in the same water that had swallowed their homes.

   My job was to find the picture. I am not proud of how quickly I began to look for
   it. I moved from room to room, framing faces in my head before I had even said
20 hello: the old man with the bandaged foot, the baby asleep on a sack of rice, the
   boy staring out at the water with his chin on his knees. Each one, I calculated,
   might make a front page. I told myself this was how the world would be persuaded to
   care. Some of that was true. Some of it was simply habit.

   It was the boy who changed things. He could not have been more than ten. When he saw
25 my camera bag he stood up, wiped his hands carefully on his shorts, and offered to
   carry it. I said no, it was heavy. He insisted, quietly, the way a host insists that
   a guest takes the best chair. So I let him. He carried it from room to room behind
   me, holding it against his chest as if it were something precious, and every time I
   stopped he set it down gently on the driest patch of floor he could find.

30 At the end of the afternoon I asked the translator to find out his name. It was
   Issa. His family's house, he said, was the third roof from the left. He pointed it
   out as if he were showing me a fine view. Then he asked, very politely, whether my
   photographs would be in a newspaper, and whether the people who saw them would know
   that Kanta had a good school and a famous football team before the water came.

35 I did not know what to say. I had spent the whole day collecting pictures of what
   Kanta had lost. Not once had it occurred to me to ask what it had been. Issa did not
   want pity. He wanted the world to know that his village had been a place worth
   living in, and that it would be again. In his eyes, I realised, I was not a rescuer
   or a witness. I was a guest who had come at a bad time.

40 I took one more photograph before the boat left. It is not the picture that went on
   the front page; my editor preferred the baby on the rice sack. It shows Issa
   standing on the school steps with my camera bag still in his arms, grinning, the
   water behind him lit gold by the late sun. I keep it on my desk. When the figures
   start to pile up, as they always do, it reminds me that every one of them has a
45 name, a house that is the third roof from the left, and a football team that is
   famous, at least, in Kanta.

### english-language.seca.cartrace.1  (45 marks)
Parts (a) to (c) are based on Text One, *The Cart Race of San Telmo*, by Hannah Wexford, in the Insert. Part (d) is based on Text Two, the extract from *A Game of Polo with a Headless Goat*, from Part 1 of the Anthology (use your own copy). Part (e) is based on both texts.
Text One: The Cart Race of San Telmo
   San Telmo clings to the side of its mountain like a cat to a curtain. Its single
   main street drops four hundred metres in a little over a kilometre, twisting back on
   itself seven times between the church at the top and the river at the bottom. On
   three hundred and sixty-four days of the year, the townspeople climb it slowly,
 5 stopping to rest on doorsteps. On the last Sunday in August, they hurl themselves
   down it in home-made wooden carts.

   I had been warned that the race started early, so I arrived at the top just after
   dawn. I need not have hurried. The church clock said seven, but nobody seemed to
   believe it. Teams sat on the steps drinking coffee and arguing about wheels. A man
10 in a helmet decorated with plastic flowers was asleep in his cart. Somebody's
   grandmother was handing out pastries from a basket, and nobody, as far as I could
   tell, was in charge.

   The carts themselves were magnificent. There was a cart shaped like a bathtub, a
   cart shaped like a fish and a cart made almost entirely from an old wardrobe, its
15 doors flapping open like wings. The rules, I was told, are simple. Four wheels. No
   engine. Brakes are recommended. 'Recommended?' I asked. Matteo, the
   eighteen-year-old captain of the wardrobe team, shrugged. 'Some people prefer not
   to,' he said. 'They are usually the winners. Or in hospital.'

   At half past nine, without any announcement I could hear, the first cart simply set
20 off. Then another, and another, at intervals that seemed to depend on nothing but
   courage. The crowd lining the street roared each one on, banging saucepans and
   blowing whistles. I ran to the third bend, where the street narrows between two
   stone walls and turns so sharply that the houses almost touch.

   The fish arrived first, rattling and shrieking, its two riders crouched low and
25 screaming instructions at each other. It hit the bend far too fast, slid sideways in
   a shower of sparks and somehow straightened again, missing the wall by the width of
   a hand. The crowd gasped and then cheered as if a goal had been scored. Behind it
   came the bathtub, which did not straighten, and which ended the race upside down in
   a flower bed, its riders climbing out to bow to tremendous applause.

30 And then the wardrobe. I heard it before I saw it: a deep, wooden thunder bouncing
   off the walls. Matteo and his cousin were wedged inside, their faces white with
   concentration, the doors still flapping. They took the bend on two wheels. For a
   moment the whole thing hung in the air, tilting, and I was sure it was going over.
   Then it crashed back down on all four and vanished round the corner, trailing a long
35 cheer behind it like a banner.

   It won, of course. When I finally reached the bottom of the hill, out of breath and
   slightly deafened, the wardrobe was parked by the river with a garland of flowers on
   its roof, and Matteo was being carried around on the shoulders of his friends. The
   prize, he told me proudly, was a ham, a trophy and the right to start first next
40 year. 'And the hospital?' I asked. He grinned and showed me a bandage on his elbow.
   'Only a little bit,' he said.

   That evening the whole town ate together at long tables in the square, and the
   arguments about wheels began all over again. I asked an old man why San Telmo had
   kept the race going for over sixty years. He thought about it for a long time. 'The
45 rest of the year,' he said, 'the hill wins. One day a year, we win.'

### english-language.seca.chess.3  (45 marks)
Parts (a) to (c) are based on Text One, *The Chess Prize*, by Lin Mei-hua, in the Insert. Part (d) is based on Text Two, the extract from *Chinese Cinderella*, from Part 1 of the Anthology (use your own copy). Part (e) is based on both texts.
Text One: The Chess Prize
   My grandfather believed in three things: hard work, cold baths and silence. He had
   been a railway engineer for forty years, and he ran our small flat as if it were a
   timetable. Breakfast was at six fifteen. Homework was checked at seven. Lights went
   out at nine, and if he heard me turning the pages of a book after that, he would
 5 knock once on the wall, very softly, which was worse than any shout.

   He rarely spoke to me except to give instructions. I do not think he disliked me. I
   think he simply did not know what to do with a girl of eleven who had arrived in his
   life with one suitcase after my parents' accident. So he did what he knew. He fed
   me, he paid for my school, and in the evenings he sat by the window with his
10 chessboard, playing against himself, his face as still as a closed door.

   I learned the game by watching. For months I sat across the room pretending to read,
   while in fact I followed every move. I learned how the knight jumps and the bishop
   slides, how a pawn that reaches the far side can become a queen. I never asked him
   to teach me. I was afraid he would say no, and even more afraid he would say yes and
15 find that I was stupid.

   When the school announced a chess tournament, I put my name down without telling
   anyone. I practised in secret on a board I had drawn on the back of a cereal box,
   with buttons for pieces. On the morning of the tournament I told my grandfather I
   was going to the library. He looked at me over his glasses for a long moment and
20 said nothing, which was what he usually said.

   The hall was full of boys. They were older than me, and louder, and most of them had
   real chess sets in wooden boxes. My first opponent laughed when he saw me sit down
   and asked if I was lost. I beat him in twenty-three moves. I beat the second boy,
   and the third. By the afternoon a small crowd had begun to gather around my table,
25 and my hands were shaking so badly that I had to hold the pieces with both fingers
   to stop them falling over.

   I lost the final. I made a careless move with my queen, saw the mistake a second
   after I had let go of the piece, and watched my opponent close in on my king with a
   terrible politeness. I shook his hand and walked to the side of the hall, determined
30 not to cry in front of everyone. That was when I saw him. My grandfather was
   standing at the back, by the fire exit, in his good coat. I had no idea how long he
   had been there.

   We walked home together without speaking. I carried my certificate for second place
   rolled up in my fist, and I was sure he was angry that I had lied about the library.
35 At the door of our building he stopped, and for a moment I thought he was going to
   knock on the wall, the way he did at night. Instead he said, 'Your queen. Move
   seventeen. You were too quick.' Then, after a pause, 'Everything else was correct.'

   It was the longest speech he had ever made to me. That evening, when homework had
   been checked, he did not go to the window. He set the board up on the kitchen table,
40 turned it so that the white pieces faced me, and waited. We played every night after
   that for nine years. He never once let me win. I have never received a prize I
   valued more than those four words: everything else was correct.

### english-language.seca.camels.2  (45 marks)
Parts (a) to (c) are based on Text One, *A Morning at the Camel Races*, by Nadia Farouk, in the Insert. Part (d) is based on Text Two, the extract from *A Game of Polo with a Headless Goat*, from Part 1 of the Anthology (use your own copy). Part (e) is based on both texts.
Text One: A Morning at the Camel Races
   The race track is a long loop of raked sand on the edge of town, marked out with
   white posts and nothing else. There are no stands. Spectators bring their own
   chairs, or sit on the bonnets of their cars, or simply stand in the soft sand at the
   side of the track with their scarves pulled up against the dust. By six in the
 5 morning, when I arrived, several hundred people were already there, and the air
   smelt of strong coffee, engine oil and camel.

   The camels themselves were waiting in a pen near the start, and they were not what I
   had expected. I had pictured slow, grumpy creatures with drooping lips. These were
   racing camels: tall, slim and long-legged, their coats brushed until they shone like
10 polished wood. Each wore a brightly coloured blanket and a muzzle, and they gazed
   down at the crowd with an expression of enormous superiority, like duchesses at a
   village fair.

   My guide was Khalid, a retired trainer of about seventy who had agreed to explain
   the sport to me. He had owned racing camels for fifty years and spoke about them the
15 way some people speak about their grandchildren. 'That one is lazy,' he said,
   pointing. 'Very talented, but lazy. That one is brave. And that one,' he lowered his
   voice, 'is a genius. But she knows it.'

   Nobody rides the camels any more. Instead, a small robot jockey is strapped to each
   animal's hump, a box of electronics no bigger than a shoebox with a little whip on
20 an arm. The owners control them by radio while driving alongside the track in their
   cars. When I asked Khalid whether he missed the old days, he was quiet for a moment.
   'The camels do not miss them,' he said at last. 'The robots are lighter. And they
   never argue.'

   The start, when it came, was sudden. A rope dropped, and twenty camels burst forward
25 in a great rolling surge of legs and dust, their long necks stretched out, their
   strides so enormous that they seemed to float above the ground. At the same moment
   every car on the road beside the track roared into life. Within seconds, there were
   two races: the camels on the sand and, alongside them, a chaotic convoy of
   four-wheel-drive vehicles, horns blaring, owners leaning out of the windows and
30 shouting instructions into their radios.

   Khalid insisted that we join them. I found myself in the back seat of his nephew's
   car, holding on to the door handle with both hands as we bounced along at sixty
   kilometres an hour, a few metres from the running camels. Dust poured in through the
   open windows. Khalid, in the front, was shouting encouragement at a camel that did
35 not belong to him and had no idea he existed. I have never been so frightened or so
   happy at the same time.

   The genius won. She crossed the line nearly two lengths clear, her robot jockey
   still flicking its tiny whip, and slowed to a dignified walk as if the whole
   business had been slightly beneath her. Her owner, a young man in mirrored
40 sunglasses, leapt from his car and danced in the sand. Several people hugged Khalid,
   although he had nothing to do with it.

   Afterwards, over more coffee, I asked him what he loved most about the races. I
   expected him to talk about speed, or money, or the thrill of winning. Instead he
   looked out at the camels, who were being led back to their pens, blankets steaming
45 in the rising heat. 'My grandfather raced camels,' he said. 'And his grandfather.
   The cars are new. The robots are new. But the camels are the same. When I watch them
   run, I am watching my family.'
