### english-language.q1.cliffharvest.1  (2 marks)
The following question is based on Text One, *The Cliff Harvest*, by Freya Lindqvist, in the Insert.
Text One: The Cliff Harvest
   From the deck of the supply boat, Skarra looks like a loaf of dark bread that
   someone has dropped into the sea. It rises sheer on every side, three hundred metres
   of black rock streaked white with the droppings of a million birds. The noise
   reaches you long before the island does: a ceaseless, rattling clamour, like a
 5 stadium full of people all talking at once in a language you do not know.

   Forty-two people live here through the winter. In summer the population doubles, as
   families return from the mainland for the few weeks when the seabirds nest. I had
   come to see what the islanders call the cliff harvest, a tradition that is at least
   eight hundred years old. For a fortnight each year, the men and some of the women go
10 over the edge of the cliffs on ropes to collect young birds from their ledges. The
   birds are salted, smoked and stored. For centuries, they were what stood between
   Skarra and starvation.

   On the first morning, I followed Hallvard, a retired fisherman of seventy-one, up
   the steep path to the cliff top. He walked with his hands clasped behind his back,
15 as if he were strolling through a park, while I stumbled and gasped behind him. At
   the top, the wind hit us like a shove from a large, impatient animal. Below, the sea
   boiled white around the rocks. Hallvard pointed out where his father had fallen,
   sixty years earlier. He did so calmly, the way you might point out where a shop used
   to be.

20 The ropes were already in place, fixed to iron stakes that had been driven into the
   turf generations ago. A young woman named Eyrún clipped herself on, checked the knot
   twice, and stepped backwards into nothing. I lay flat on my stomach and peered over
   the edge, my fingers dug into the grass. She was swinging gently against the rock
   face, sixty metres down, using a long pole with a net on the end to scoop birds from
25 the ledges. Above and below her, thousands of adult birds wheeled and screamed.
   Every so often she pushed off from the cliff with her feet and drifted out over the
   drop, and every time she did so my stomach turned over.

   I had expected to feel uneasy, and I did. The young birds are plump, grey and
   fluffy, and they make a soft, complaining whistle as they are lifted from the nest.
30 On the mainland, where I live, people pay to watch them through telescopes. Here
   they are food. I found myself silently urging each chick to shuffle back into a
   crack where the net could not reach it, and at the same time willing Eyrún to be
   quick, to be safe, to come back up.

   That evening, in Hallvard's kitchen, I tried to explain how I felt. He listened
35 politely, pouring coffee so strong it was almost solid. Then he told me that the
   islanders take only a small proportion of the young birds each year, from the same
   ledges their grandparents used, and that the colony is larger now than it was when
   he was a boy. Scientists from the university come every spring to count the nests.
   'They count, we eat,' he said, and laughed. 'Both of us want the same thing. We want
40 the birds to come back.'

   I am still not sure what I think. It is easy, from a distance, to be sentimental
   about wild creatures. It is harder when you have eaten the islanders' bread, slept
   under their roof and watched a young woman risk her life on a rope for food that her
   family will rely on through the long, dark months when no boat can land. What I do
45 know is that I have never seen people more closely bound to a place, or more
   respectful of the creatures they share it with.

   On my last morning, the supply boat arrived early. As we pulled away, the noise of
   the colony followed us out to sea, gradually fading until it was no louder than the
   hiss of the waves. I watched the island shrink to a dark smudge on the horizon.
50 Somewhere on those cliffs, I knew, Eyrún was already clipping herself on to the rope
   again.
 Look at lines 2–3. Select **two** words or phrases from these lines that describe the island. [2 mark(s)]

### english-language.q1.chess.3  (2 marks)
The following question is based on Text One, *The Chess Prize*, by Lin Mei-hua, in the Insert.
Text One: The Chess Prize
   My grandfather believed in three things: hard work, cold baths and silence. He had
   been a railway engineer for forty years, and he ran our small flat as if it were a
   timetable. Breakfast was at six fifteen. Homework was checked at seven. Lights went
   out at nine, and if he heard me turning the pages of a book after that, he would
 5 knock once on the wall, very softly, which was worse than any shout.

   He rarely spoke to me except to give instructions. I do not think he disliked me. I
   think he simply did not know what to do with a girl of eleven who had arrived in his
   life with one suitcase after my parents' accident. So he did what he knew. He fed
   me, he paid for my school, and in the evenings he sat by the window with his
10 chessboard, playing against himself, his face as still as a closed door.

   I learned the game by watching. For months I sat across the room pretending to read,
   while in fact I followed every move. I learned how the knight jumps and the bishop
   slides, how a pawn that reaches the far side can become a queen. I never asked him
   to teach me. I was afraid he would say no, and even more afraid he would say yes and
15 find that I was stupid.

   When the school announced a chess tournament, I put my name down without telling
   anyone. I practised in secret on a board I had drawn on the back of a cereal box,
   with buttons for pieces. On the morning of the tournament I told my grandfather I
   was going to the library. He looked at me over his glasses for a long moment and
20 said nothing, which was what he usually said.

   The hall was full of boys. They were older than me, and louder, and most of them had
   real chess sets in wooden boxes. My first opponent laughed when he saw me sit down
   and asked if I was lost. I beat him in twenty-three moves. I beat the second boy,
   and the third. By the afternoon a small crowd had begun to gather around my table,
25 and my hands were shaking so badly that I had to hold the pieces with both fingers
   to stop them falling over.

   I lost the final. I made a careless move with my queen, saw the mistake a second
   after I had let go of the piece, and watched my opponent close in on my king with a
   terrible politeness. I shook his hand and walked to the side of the hall, determined
30 not to cry in front of everyone. That was when I saw him. My grandfather was
   standing at the back, by the fire exit, in his good coat. I had no idea how long he
   had been there.

   We walked home together without speaking. I carried my certificate for second place
   rolled up in my fist, and I was sure he was angry that I had lied about the library.
35 At the door of our building he stopped, and for a moment I thought he was going to
   knock on the wall, the way he did at night. Instead he said, 'Your queen. Move
   seventeen. You were too quick.' Then, after a pause, 'Everything else was correct.'

   It was the longest speech he had ever made to me. That evening, when homework had
   been checked, he did not go to the window. He set the board up on the kitchen table,
40 turned it so that the white pieces faced me, and waited. We played every night after
   that for nine years. He never once let me win. I have never received a prize I
   valued more than those four words: everything else was correct.
 Look at lines 8–10. Select **two** words or phrases from these lines that describe the grandfather's evenings. [2 mark(s)]

### english-language.q1.afterwater.3  (2 marks)
The following question is based on Text One, *After the Water*, by Tomás Reyes, in the Insert.
Text One: After the Water
   The boat smelt of diesel and wet rope. For three hours we nosed between the tops of
   trees, the engine coughing whenever the propeller caught on something hidden beneath
   the brown water. Somewhere under us, the boatman told me, was the road we would
   normally have driven along. I tried to imagine it: the tarmac, the lorries, the
 5 stalls selling roasted corn. All I could see was water, flat and sullen, stretching
   to a sky that looked as though it had been scrubbed with ash.

   I had covered floods before. I knew what to expect, or thought I did. In my bag were
   three cameras, eleven lenses and a notebook already half full of figures: the number
   of villages cut off, the number of families displaced, the tonnes of grain lost.
10 Figures are what editors ask for first. They fit neatly into a headline. They do not
   smell, or cry, or ask you for help.

   We reached Kanta just after midday. It had been a village of perhaps four hundred
   people. Now it was an island of rooftops and one concrete school building, its upper
   floor crowded with everyone who had nowhere else to go. Washing hung from the
15 windows. A goat stood on a corrugated roof, bleating at the water as if it could
   argue it away. On the steps that led down into the flood, a line of women were
   rinsing pots in the same water that had swallowed their homes.

   My job was to find the picture. I am not proud of how quickly I began to look for
   it. I moved from room to room, framing faces in my head before I had even said
20 hello: the old man with the bandaged foot, the baby asleep on a sack of rice, the
   boy staring out at the water with his chin on his knees. Each one, I calculated,
   might make a front page. I told myself this was how the world would be persuaded to
   care. Some of that was true. Some of it was simply habit.

   It was the boy who changed things. He could not have been more than ten. When he saw
25 my camera bag he stood up, wiped his hands carefully on his shorts, and offered to
   carry it. I said no, it was heavy. He insisted, quietly, the way a host insists that
   a guest takes the best chair. So I let him. He carried it from room to room behind
   me, holding it against his chest as if it were something precious, and every time I
   stopped he set it down gently on the driest patch of floor he could find.

30 At the end of the afternoon I asked the translator to find out his name. It was
   Issa. His family's house, he said, was the third roof from the left. He pointed it
   out as if he were showing me a fine view. Then he asked, very politely, whether my
   photographs would be in a newspaper, and whether the people who saw them would know
   that Kanta had a good school and a famous football team before the water came.

35 I did not know what to say. I had spent the whole day collecting pictures of what
   Kanta had lost. Not once had it occurred to me to ask what it had been. Issa did not
   want pity. He wanted the world to know that his village had been a place worth
   living in, and that it would be again. In his eyes, I realised, I was not a rescuer
   or a witness. I was a guest who had come at a bad time.

40 I took one more photograph before the boat left. It is not the picture that went on
   the front page; my editor preferred the baby on the rice sack. It shows Issa
   standing on the school steps with my camera bag still in his arms, grinning, the
   water behind him lit gold by the late sun. I keep it on my desk. When the figures
   start to pile up, as they always do, it reminds me that every one of them has a
45 name, a house that is the third roof from the left, and a football team that is
   famous, at least, in Kanta.
 Look at lines 14–16. Select **two** words or phrases from these lines that describe what the writer sees in the village. [2 mark(s)]

### english-language.q1.postcard.1  (2 marks)
The following question is based on Text One, *The Town on the Postcard*, by Mira Castellanos, in the Insert.
Text One: The Town on the Postcard
   When people ask where I am from, I have learned to watch their faces. I say the name
   of my town, and there is a small pause, a polite tightening around the mouth, and
   then the question that always follows: 'Isn't that the place with the mine?' Yes, I
   tell them. It is the place with the mine. It is also the place with the best bread I
 5 have ever eaten, a river that turns copper-coloured at sunset, and a brass band that
   has played every Sunday for a hundred and twelve years. But nobody asks about the
   band.

   For a long time I did not mention it either. As a child, I believed what the
   postcards in the station kiosk told me: that our town was a grey smudge on the edge
10 of the map, a place you passed through on the way to somewhere better. The postcards
   showed the winding tower, the slag heaps and the long, low rows of miners' cottages
   under a heavy sky. Not one of them showed a person smiling. I used to think the
   photographer had simply been unlucky with the weather. Now I think he had already
   decided what he wanted to see.

15 When I was eleven, our school took part in an exchange with a school in the capital.
   Thirty of us travelled by coach for six hours, our faces pressed to the windows as
   the hills flattened and the motorways multiplied. The city was enormous and
   glittering, and I loved it at once. I loved the glass towers, the noise, the shops
   that sold forty kinds of tea. What I did not love was the way my host family spoke
20 to me.

   They were kind. I want to be clear about that. Mrs Adeyemi-Hart cooked a special
   dinner on my first night and gave me a toothbrush in case 'we didn't have them where
   I came from'. Her son asked whether I had ever seen a cinema. When I said that my
   uncle ran one, he looked at me as if I had told him my uncle ran a spaceship. They
25 had a picture of my town in their heads, and the picture had no room in it for
   cinemas, or toothbrushes, or me.

   I came home angry. I remember storming into the kitchen and telling my grandmother
   that the city people thought we lived in caves. She was rolling pastry at the table,
   and she did not stop. 'And what do you think of the city people?' she asked. I told
30 her they were rude, and spoilt, and that they all ignored each other on the trains.
   She laughed so hard that flour rose from her hands like smoke. 'So,' she said, 'now
   you have a postcard of them.'

   It took me years to understand what she meant. I had gone to the city expecting to
   be judged, and I had been. But I had also arrived with my own small, crumpled
35 picture of city people, and I had spent ten days collecting evidence to prove it
   right. Every unsmiling commuter went into my collection. Every friendly shopkeeper,
   every boy who offered me his seat, I quietly threw away.

   This is the trouble with a single picture of a place. It is not exactly false. Our
   town does have a mine, and the mine has scarred the hills. The city does have
40 crowded trains full of tired people staring at their phones. The postcard is true,
   as far as it goes. The danger is that it does not go very far, and that we are lazy
   enough to let it stand in for the whole.

   Last summer I went back to the kiosk at the station. The postcards are still there,
   curling slightly at the corners, the same grey tower under the same grey sky. So I
45 did something my grandmother would have approved of. I borrowed my father's camera
   and spent a week taking new pictures: the baker sliding loaves out of the oven at
   four in the morning, the band tuning up in the square, the children diving off the
   old bridge into the copper river. I had them printed and asked the kiosk owner if
   she would sell them. She looked at them for a long time. Then she cleared a whole
50 shelf.

   I am not asking you to love my town. I am asking you to notice how quickly you
   decide what a place is like, or what a person is like, from one image you did not
   even choose. Look for the second postcard. Then look for the third. You will be
   surprised how much of the world is hiding behind the first.
 Look at lines 4–6. Select **two** words or phrases from these lines that show what the writer values about her town. [2 mark(s)]
