    LLevel 4 8-10: Thorough exploration of how language and structure create effects, including vocabulary, sentence structure and other features. References are well chosen, detailed and fully support the points made.

    • he is self-critical about journalism, comparing the search for shocking pictures to a craving for a drug, which is honest and unsettling
    • he admits to revulsion, then explains why reports avoid saying so; breaking this taboo makes his account feel truthful
    • a rhetorical question asks how he should feel, standing there strong and confident, inviting the reader to share his discomfort
    • the ending addresses the 'nameless friend' directly, closing on regret, gratitude and a colloquial «I owe you one»
    • the opening list of adjectives describing a thousand faces conveys the scale of suffering, then narrows to the one face he will never forget, creating curiosity
    • the notebook directions to the village, written in note form, add authenticity and suggest how remote and forgotten the place is
    • the story of Amina and her daughters gives names and ages, making the victims individual people rather than statistics
    • short, stark statements (one daughter had died) are placed bluntly, so the shock lands on the reader as it did on him
    • sensory detail, especially smell, makes the scene of the old woman's wound vivid and disturbing

    LLevel 4 14-18: Makes a wide range of comparisons. Explores the writers' ideas and perspectives and how theme, language and/or structure are used across both texts. References are balanced between the texts and fully support the points.
    LLevel 5 19-22: Makes varied, wide-ranging and sustained comparisons. Analyses the writers' ideas and perspectives and how theme, language and/or structure are used across both texts. References are balanced, discriminating and fully support the points.
    • Reward responses that compare how the writers of Text One (Tomás Reyes) and Text Two (George Alagiah) present their ideas and perspectives about the people they meet while reporting. Responses may include some of the following points:
    • both texts are first-person accounts by journalists reporting on a humanitarian disaster: famine in Text Two, a flood in Text One
    • both writers establish the scale of suffering before focusing on one individual who matters most
    • both are self-critical about the media's hunger for shocking images: Alagiah compares it to a drug, Reyes admits he is 'not proud'
    • both suggest statistics and repeated images hide real people
    • both describe the dignity of people in desperate situations: the man keeping his hoe in Text Two, Issa wiping his hands before carrying the bag in Text One
    • both centre on one brief encounter that changes the writer's perspective: the smile in Text Two, the boy carrying the bag in Text One
    • in both, the encounter reverses the usual relationship: Alagiah's subject 'turns the tables', and Reyes realises he is a guest rather than a rescuer
    • Alagiah never learns the man's name, whereas Reyes does find out Issa's name and stresses that every figure has a name
    • Text Two contains disturbing physical detail of disease and death and openly admits revulsion; Text One is gentler, focusing on lost homes and hope
    • the encounter in Text Two is almost wordless, while in Text One Issa speaks to the writer through a translator
    • both end with personal reflection on the purpose of journalism: Alagiah resolves to write with power, Reyes keeps a photograph as a reminder
    • Text Two ends with regret and a direct address to his 'nameless friend'; Text One ends with affectionate humour about the football team
