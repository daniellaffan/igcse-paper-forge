### english-literature.unseen-kite.0002  (20 marks)
Read the following poem.
Kite
My father made it from a bin bag,
two garden canes and sellotape
and a tail of my mother's tights.
It was ugly. It was beautiful.

On the hill the wind was waiting.
He ran, I ran, the string burned,
and then the kite jumped out of his hands
the way a fish jumps out of a bucket,

and climbed, and climbed,
a black diamond above the town,
shrugging and pulling at the string
as if it wanted to be somewhere else.

He gave the reel to me.
I felt the sky tugging at my arm,
the whole weight of the wind
wanting me as well.

He said, Don't let go. I didn't.
But I understood that day
that some things are made
to be held only so tightly.

### english-literature.unseen-last-shift.0003  (20 marks)
Read the following poem.
The Last Shift
The siren went at six, as always,
but this time nobody came in.
Instead we stood outside the gate
in our good coats, like a funeral.

Forty years the furnace breathed
for this town. It paid for weddings,
football boots, a hundred holidays
by a sea that was mostly rain.

Now the chimney is a finger
pointing at nothing. The windows
have been boarded up like eyes
closed by a kind hand.

Old Davies, who lost two fingers
to the press in seventy-nine,
put his good hand on the railings
as if he were feeling for a pulse.

Nobody made a speech.
Somebody lit a cigarette.
The siren stopped, and the silence
was the loudest thing we'd heard.

### english-literature.unseen-lighthouse.0003  (20 marks)
Read the following poem.
The Lighthouse Keeper's Daughter
I grew up inside a candle
on a rock the size of a church.
Every night my father climbed
one hundred and twelve steps
to light the sea.

I learned my letters from the logbook:
WIND NW 6. FOG. ONE SHIP.
My friends were gulls with names,
a seal that came on Thursdays,
the slow tick of the lamp.

On the mainland, children ran
in gangs along the harbour wall.
I watched them through the glass
like a film with the sound off,
and wondered what they laughed at.

Now I live in a city
where the lights never go out,
and nobody looks up at them,
and some nights I climb the stairs
to my flat, counting: one hundred and twelve.
