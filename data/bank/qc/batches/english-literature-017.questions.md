### english-literature.unseen-snow-day.0001  (20 marks)
Read the following poem.
Snow Day
The radio said it: schools closed.
We cheered like a goal had been scored
and were outside before our boots were on.

The street had been rubbed out.
Cars were loaves. The postbox wore a hat.
The world was a page nobody had written on yet,

so we wrote on it: angels, footprints,
a snowman with a carrot nose
and our grandfather's terrible scarf.

We fought a war with no one hurt,
we built a slide down Mrs Patel's drive
and she came out and used it too.

At dusk our hands were red as jam
and our mother called us in
to soup and steam and socks on the radiator.

I remember nothing I learned that year
at school. I remember everything
about the day the snow came.

*Original poem written for this practice bank*

### english-literature.unseen-soft-rains.0002  (20 marks)
Read the following poem.
There Will Come Soft Rains
There will come soft rains and the smell of the ground,
And swallows circling with their shimmering sound;

And frogs in the pools singing at night,
And wild plum trees in tremulous white;

Robins will wear their feathery fire,
Whistling their whims on a low fence-wire;

And not one will know of the war, not one
Will care at last when it is done.

Not one would mind, neither bird nor tree,
If mankind perished utterly;

And Spring herself, when she woke at dawn
Would scarcely know that we were gone.

*Sara Teasdale (1918)*

### english-literature.unseen-spring-and-fall.0002  (20 marks)
Read the following poem.
Spring and Fall
to a young child

Margaret, are you grieving
Over Goldengrove unleaving*?
Leaves, like the things of man, you
With your fresh thoughts care for, can you?
Ah! as the heart grows older
It will come to such sights colder
By and by, nor spare a sigh
Though worlds of wanwood leafmeal* lie;
And yet you will weep and know why.
Now no matter, child, the name:
Sorrow's springs are the same.
Nor mouth had, no nor mind, expressed
What heart heard of, ghost guessed:
It is the blight man was born for,
It is Margaret you mourn for.

*unleaving: losing its leaves
*wanwood leafmeal: pale woods with leaves fallen and broken into pieces

*Gerard Manley Hopkins (written 1880; published 1918)*
