### history.russia-interpretation.0051  (16 marks)
Source A: From a speech by a Bolshevik leader to party members, 1919.
The Red Army must be fed, or the Whites will return and hang us all. Every pood of grain taken from the kulaks is a bullet against Kolchak and Denikin. These are hard measures, but the revolution is at war.
Source B: From the diary of a factory worker in Petrograd, 1920.
Our ration is a quarter of a pound of bread a day, and half the time it does not arrive. Many of my comrades have gone back to their villages to find food. The factory stands half empty. The commissar says it is all for the front, and we must endure until the Whites are beaten.
Extract C: From a history of the Soviet economy, published in 2009.
War Communism helped the Bolsheviks to win the Civil War, but at a terrible cost. Requisitioning fed the Red Army, yet it destroyed the peasants' will to grow food. By 1921 the cities were empty, factories were idle and revolts had broken out across the countryside.

### history.russia-interpretation.0021  (16 marks)

The situation is serious. There is anarchy in the capital. The government is paralysed. Transport and food supplies are in complete disorder. Troops are firing on one another. A person trusted by the country must be given power to form a new government at once.
Source B: From the diary of a Tsarist general at army headquarters, March 1917.
His Majesty wished to send loyal troops to the capital, but the generals advised him that the army could not be relied upon. Some of us believed he could still save the dynasty. He signed the abdication calmly, as though he were handing over command of a squadron.
Extract C: From a history of the Russian Revolution, published in 2017.
The February Revolution was not planned by any party. It grew out of bread queues and strikes, and became a revolution only when the soldiers of the Petrograd garrison refused to fire on the crowds. Within a week the three-hundred-year-old Romanov dynasty had gone.

### history.russia-interpretation.0040  (16 marks)
Source A: From the memoirs of a Socialist Revolutionary deputy to the Constituent Assembly, published in 1921.
We arrived at the Tauride Palace on 5 January to find it surrounded by sailors with rifles. The galleries were full of armed men who jeered at our speeches. When we tried to return the next day, the doors were locked. The people had voted for us, and Lenin simply sent us home.
Source B: From a letter written by a peasant to a newspaper, December 1917.
At last the land is ours! The new government has given us the landlord's estate without payment. We have divided it fairly by the number of mouths in each family. The Red Guards in town have shut the newspaper of the Socialist Revolutionaries, but what do we care for newspapers? Long live the Soviet power!
Extract C: From a study of the early Soviet state, published in 2014.
Lenin's first decrees gave the people what they had been demanding all year. Peasants received land, soldiers received peace and workers received the eight-hour day. These measures were popular and help explain why so many Russians were willing, at first, to tolerate the new government.

### history.russia-interpretation.0008  (16 marks)
Source A: From the memoirs of a liberal member of the First Duma, published in 1922.
When the Manifesto was published in October 1905, we believed that Russia had become a constitutional state at last. Our joy did not last. Within months the Tsar had issued laws that left him master of the army, the ministers and the purse. When we criticised his government, he simply sent us home.
Source B: From a government statement published in newspapers, October 1906.
His Majesty has granted his people the Duma and the freedoms of speech and assembly. Order has been restored throughout the empire. Those who used these freedoms to spread violence have been punished, and loyal subjects may now look forward to peace and progress.
Extract C: From a history of the Romanov dynasty, published in 2015.
The Tsar survived 1905 because his enemies were divided and his army stayed loyal. The October Manifesto gave the liberals what they wanted, or seemed to, and so the middle classes deserted the revolution. The workers and peasants were then crushed one group at a time. The promises of 1905 were soon taken back.
 Study Extract C.
Extract C suggests that the Tsar's concessions in 1905 were not genuine.
How far do you agree with this interpretation?
Use Extract C, Sources A and B and your own knowledge to explain your answer. [16 mark(s)]
