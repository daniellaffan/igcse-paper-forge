### english-language.q3.cartrace.1  (5 marks)
The following question is based on Text One, *The Cart Race of San Telmo*, by Hannah Wexford, in the Insert.
Text One: The Cart Race of San Telmo
   San Telmo clings to the side of its mountain like a cat to a curtain. Its single
   main street drops four hundred metres in a little over a kilometre, twisting back on
   itself seven times between the church at the top and the river at the bottom. On
   three hundred and sixty-four days of the year, the townspeople climb it slowly,
 5 stopping to rest on doorsteps. On the last Sunday in August, they hurl themselves
   down it in home-made wooden carts.

   I had been warned that the race started early, so I arrived at the top just after
   dawn. I need not have hurried. The church clock said seven, but nobody seemed to
   believe it. Teams sat on the steps drinking coffee and arguing about wheels. A man
10 in a helmet decorated with plastic flowers was asleep in his cart. Somebody's
   grandmother was handing out pastries from a basket, and nobody, as far as I could
   tell, was in charge.

   The carts themselves were magnificent. There was a cart shaped like a bathtub, a
   cart shaped like a fish and a cart made almost entirely from an old wardrobe, its
15 doors flapping open like wings. The rules, I was told, are simple. Four wheels. No
   engine. Brakes are recommended. 'Recommended?' I asked. Matteo, the
   eighteen-year-old captain of the wardrobe team, shrugged. 'Some people prefer not
   to,' he said. 'They are usually the winners. Or in hospital.'

   At half past nine, without any announcement I could hear, the first cart simply set
20 off. Then another, and another, at intervals that seemed to depend on nothing but
   courage. The crowd lining the street roared each one on, banging saucepans and
   blowing whistles. I ran to the third bend, where the street narrows between two
   stone walls and turns so sharply that the houses almost touch.

   The fish arrived first, rattling and shrieking, its two riders crouched low and
25 screaming instructions at each other. It hit the bend far too fast, slid sideways in
   a shower of sparks and somehow straightened again, missing the wall by the width of
   a hand. The crowd gasped and then cheered as if a goal had been scored. Behind it
   came the bathtub, which did not straighten, and which ended the race upside down in
   a flower bed, its riders climbing out to bow to tremendous applause.

30 And then the wardrobe. I heard it before I saw it: a deep, wooden thunder bouncing
   off the walls. Matteo and his cousin were wedged inside, their faces white with
   concentration, the doors still flapping. They took the bend on two wheels. For a
   moment the whole thing hung in the air, tilting, and I was sure it was going over.
   Then it crashed back down on all four and vanished round the corner, trailing a long
35 cheer behind it like a banner.

   It won, of course. When I finally reached the bottom of the hill, out of breath and
   slightly deafened, the wardrobe was parked by the river with a garland of flowers on
   its roof, and Matteo was being carried around on the shoulders of his friends. The
   prize, he told me proudly, was a ham, a trophy and the right to start first next
40 year. 'And the hospital?' I asked. He grinned and showed me a bandage on his elbow.
   'Only a little bit,' he said.

   That evening the whole town ate together at long tables in the square, and the
   arguments about wheels began all over again. I asked an old man why San Telmo had
   kept the race going for over sixty years. He thought about it for a long time. 'The
45 rest of the year,' he said, 'the hill wins. One day a year, we win.'

### english-language.q3.cliffharvest.2  (5 marks)
The following question is based on Text One, *The Cliff Harvest*, by Freya Lindqvist, in the Insert.
Text One: The Cliff Harvest
   From the deck of the supply boat, Skarra looks like a loaf of dark bread that
   someone has dropped into the sea. It rises sheer on every side, three hundred metres
   of black rock streaked white with the droppings of a million birds. The noise
   reaches you long before the island does: a ceaseless, rattling clamour, like a
 5 stadium full of people all talking at once in a language you do not know.

   Forty-two people live here through the winter. In summer the population doubles, as
   families return from the mainland for the few weeks when the seabirds nest. I had
   come to see what the islanders call the cliff harvest, a tradition that is at least
   eight hundred years old. For a fortnight each year, the men and some of the women go
10 over the edge of the cliffs on ropes to collect young birds from their ledges. The
   birds are salted, smoked and stored. For centuries, they were what stood between
   Skarra and starvation.

   On the first morning, I followed Hallvard, a retired fisherman of seventy-one, up
   the steep path to the cliff top. He walked with his hands clasped behind his back,
15 as if he were strolling through a park, while I stumbled and gasped behind him. At
   the top, the wind hit us like a shove from a large, impatient animal. Below, the sea
   boiled white around the rocks. Hallvard pointed out where his father had fallen,
   sixty years earlier. He did so calmly, the way you might point out where a shop used
   to be.

20 The ropes were already in place, fixed to iron stakes that had been driven into the
   turf generations ago. A young woman named Eyrún clipped herself on, checked the knot
   twice, and stepped backwards into nothing. I lay flat on my stomach and peered over
   the edge, my fingers dug into the grass. She was swinging gently against the rock
   face, sixty metres down, using a long pole with a net on the end to scoop birds from
25 the ledges. Above and below her, thousands of adult birds wheeled and screamed.
   Every so often she pushed off from the cliff with her feet and drifted out over the
   drop, and every time she did so my stomach turned over.

   I had expected to feel uneasy, and I did. The young birds are plump, grey and
   fluffy, and they make a soft, complaining whistle as they are lifted from the nest.
30 On the mainland, where I live, people pay to watch them through telescopes. Here
   they are food. I found myself silently urging each chick to shuffle back into a
   crack where the net could not reach it, and at the same time willing Eyrún to be
   quick, to be safe, to come back up.

   That evening, in Hallvard's kitchen, I tried to explain how I felt. He listened
35 politely, pouring coffee so strong it was almost solid. Then he told me that the
   islanders take only a small proportion of the young birds each year, from the same
   ledges their grandparents used, and that the colony is larger now than it was when
   he was a boy. Scientists from the university come every spring to count the nests.
   'They count, we eat,' he said, and laughed. 'Both of us want the same thing. We want
40 the birds to come back.'

   I am still not sure what I think. It is easy, from a distance, to be sentimental
   about wild creatures. It is harder when you have eaten the islanders' bread, slept
   under their roof and watched a young woman risk her life on a rope for food that her
   family will rely on through the long, dark months when no boat can land. What I do
45 know is that I have never seen people more closely bound to a place, or more
   respectful of the creatures they share it with.

   On my last morning, the supply boat arrived early. As we pulled away, the noise of
   the colony followed us out to sea, gradually fading until it was no louder than the
   hiss of the waves. I watched the island shrink to a dark smudge on the horizon.
50 Somewhere on those cliffs, I knew, Eyrún was already clipping herself on to the rope
   again.
 From lines 41–51, explain what the writer has learned from her visit.

### english-language.q3.stammer.2  (5 marks)
The following question is based on Text One, *The Voice on the Radio*, by Priya Lal, in the Insert.
Text One: The Voice on the Radio
   Every weekday at six o'clock, about half a million people wake up to Danny Okoro's
   voice. It is warm, quick and full of jokes, the kind of voice that makes you feel
   the day might not be so bad after all. What most of his listeners do not know is
   that, until he was nineteen, Danny could not say his own name without getting stuck.

 5 'D was the worst,' he tells me, stirring three sugars into his coffee in a café near
   the studio. 'Which is unlucky, when your name is Danny. I used to introduce myself
   as Dan-Dan-Dan until people just started calling me that. Some of them meant it
   kindly. Most of them didn't.'

   School, he says, was a daily battle. He would count the pupils ahead of him when the
10 teacher took the register, rehearsing the word 'here' over and over in his head
   until his turn came, and then it would refuse to come out. He learned to swap
   difficult words for easier ones, so that his sentences became strange and
   roundabout. One teacher wrote on his report that he was 'bright but lazy in oral
   work'. Another made him read aloud in front of the class 'to build his confidence'.
15 He describes that lesson as the longest twenty minutes of his life.

   'I wasn't angry with them,' he says now. 'They didn't understand it. Nobody did
   then. They thought if I just tried harder, or relaxed, or breathed properly, it
   would go away. That's like telling someone with a broken leg to walk more
   confidently.'

20 The turning point came in an unlikely place: a hospital radio station, where his
   older cousin volunteered. Danny went along one evening to help carry boxes and ended
   up being handed a microphone when a presenter failed to turn up. 'I was terrified,'
   he says. 'But there was something about it. Nobody could see me. Nobody was looking
   at my face waiting for me to finish. It was just me and the music and these patients
25 lying in bed who were glad of a bit of company.' He stammered through the whole
   hour. A nurse rang in afterwards to say it was the best show they had had in months.

   He went back the next week, and the next. With the help of a speech therapist, he
   learned techniques for easing into difficult sounds, and, just as importantly, he
   learned to stop being ashamed of the moments when they did not work. He still
30 stammers occasionally on air. He does not edit it out. 'If I hide it, I'm telling
   every kid who stammers that it's something to hide,' he says. 'I'm not doing that.'

   Letters arrive at the studio every week, many of them from young listeners. One boy
   wrote to say he had answered the register for the first time after hearing Danny
   talk about it. Danny keeps that letter in his wallet. He takes it out to show me,
35 the paper soft from folding. 'That's worth more than any award,' he says.

   As we leave the café, a woman at the next table recognises his voice and asks for a
   photograph. Danny chats to her easily, and when he gets stuck for a moment on the
   word 'pleasure', he simply waits, smiles, and lets the word arrive. She does not
   seem to notice. Neither, I realise, do I.
 From lines 1–15, explain what we learn about Danny's life before and after he became a presenter.

### english-language.q3.chess.2  (5 marks)
The following question is based on Text One, *The Chess Prize*, by Lin Mei-hua, in the Insert.
Text One: The Chess Prize
   My grandfather believed in three things: hard work, cold baths and silence. He had
   been a railway engineer for forty years, and he ran our small flat as if it were a
   timetable. Breakfast was at six fifteen. Homework was checked at seven. Lights went
   out at nine, and if he heard me turning the pages of a book after that, he would
 5 knock once on the wall, very softly, which was worse than any shout.

   He rarely spoke to me except to give instructions. I do not think he disliked me. I
   think he simply did not know what to do with a girl of eleven who had arrived in his
   life with one suitcase after my parents' accident. So he did what he knew. He fed
   me, he paid for my school, and in the evenings he sat by the window with his
10 chessboard, playing against himself, his face as still as a closed door.

   I learned the game by watching. For months I sat across the room pretending to read,
   while in fact I followed every move. I learned how the knight jumps and the bishop
   slides, how a pawn that reaches the far side can become a queen. I never asked him
   to teach me. I was afraid he would say no, and even more afraid he would say yes and
15 find that I was stupid.

   When the school announced a chess tournament, I put my name down without telling
   anyone. I practised in secret on a board I had drawn on the back of a cereal box,
   with buttons for pieces. On the morning of the tournament I told my grandfather I
   was going to the library. He looked at me over his glasses for a long moment and
20 said nothing, which was what he usually said.

   The hall was full of boys. They were older than me, and louder, and most of them had
   real chess sets in wooden boxes. My first opponent laughed when he saw me sit down
   and asked if I was lost. I beat him in twenty-three moves. I beat the second boy,
   and the third. By the afternoon a small crowd had begun to gather around my table,
25 and my hands were shaking so badly that I had to hold the pieces with both fingers
   to stop them falling over.

   I lost the final. I made a careless move with my queen, saw the mistake a second
   after I had let go of the piece, and watched my opponent close in on my king with a
   terrible politeness. I shook his hand and walked to the side of the hall, determined
30 not to cry in front of everyone. That was when I saw him. My grandfather was
   standing at the back, by the fire exit, in his good coat. I had no idea how long he
   had been there.

   We walked home together without speaking. I carried my certificate for second place
   rolled up in my fist, and I was sure he was angry that I had lied about the library.
35 At the door of our building he stopped, and for a moment I thought he was going to
   knock on the wall, the way he did at night. Instead he said, 'Your queen. Move
   seventeen. You were too quick.' Then, after a pause, 'Everything else was correct.'

   It was the longest speech he had ever made to me. That evening, when homework had
   been checked, he did not go to the window. He set the board up on the kitchen table,
40 turned it so that the white pieces faced me, and waited. We played every night after
   that for nine years. He never once let me win. I have never received a prize I
   valued more than those four words: everything else was correct.
 From lines 1–10, explain what we learn about the writer's grandfather.

You may support your points with brief quotations. [5 mark(s)]
