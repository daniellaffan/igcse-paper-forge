### english-literature.unseen-swimming-lesson.0002  (20 marks)
Read the following poem.
The Swimming Lesson
You said, Lie back. I've got you.
Your hand flat under my spine
like a tray carrying something
that might spill.

The water filled my ears
with a roar like a shell's.
The ceiling lights swam.
I was sure that I would sink.

Breathe, you said. Just breathe.
Your voice came from far off,
the other side of a wall of water.
I breathed. I floated.

I did not know
for a full minute
that you had taken your hand away
and were standing back, watching,

your arms half-raised
in case, your face
doing the thing that faces do
when they are proud and frightened

at the same time.

### english-literature.unseen-sympathy.0001  (20 marks)
Read the following poem.
Sympathy
I know what the caged bird feels, alas!
When the sun is bright on the upland slopes;
When the wind stirs soft through the springing grass,
And the river flows like a stream of glass;
When the first bird sings and the first bud opes*,
And the faint perfume from its chalice steals -
I know what the caged bird feels!

I know why the caged bird beats his wing
Till its blood is red on the cruel bars;
For he must fly back to his perch and cling
When he fain* would be on the bough a-swing;
And a pain still throbs in the old, old scars
And they pulse again with a keener sting -
I know why he beats his wing!

I know why the caged bird sings, ah me,
When his wing is bruised and his bosom sore, -
When he beats his bars and he would be free;
It is not a carol of joy or glee,
But a prayer that he sends from his heart's deep core,
But a plea, that upward to Heaven he flings -
I know why the caged bird sings!

*opes: opens
*fain: gladly

*Paul Laurence Dunbar (1899)*
 Explore how the writer presents freedom and captivity in this poem.

### english-literature.unseen-the-allotment.0003  (20 marks)
Read the following poem.
The Allotment
Every Sunday my father took me
to the square of earth he rented
behind the railway line,

and showed me how to push a bean
into the dark with one finger,
how deep, how far apart,

how to tie the tomatoes
with strips of his old shirts,
how to wait.

He did not talk much there.
He talked with his hands, with the fork,
with the slow way he knelt.

Trains went by full of people
going somewhere faster.
We stayed. We watched things grow.

Now the plot is someone else's,
and he is ninety, and forgets
my name some days, but not

the beans: he lifts one finger
and pushes it into the air,
how deep, how far apart.
