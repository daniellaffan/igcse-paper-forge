### english-language.q5.afterwater  (22 marks)
This question is based on both Text One, *After the Water*, in the Insert, and Text Two, the extract from *A Passage to Africa*, from Part 1 of the Anthology.
Text One: After the Water
   The boat smelt of diesel and wet rope. For three hours we nosed between the tops of
   trees, the engine coughing whenever the propeller caught on something hidden beneath
   the brown water. Somewhere under us, the boatman told me, was the road we would
   normally have driven along. I tried to imagine it: the tarmac, the lorries, the
 5 stalls selling roasted corn. All I could see was water, flat and sullen, stretching
   to a sky that looked as though it had been scrubbed with ash.

   I had covered floods before. I knew what to expect, or thought I did. In my bag were
   three cameras, eleven lenses and a notebook already half full of figures: the number
   of villages cut off, the number of families displaced, the tonnes of grain lost.
10 Figures are what editors ask for first. They fit neatly into a headline. They do not
   smell, or cry, or ask you for help.

   We reached Kanta just after midday. It had been a village of perhaps four hundred
   people. Now it was an island of rooftops and one concrete school building, its upper
   floor crowded with everyone who had nowhere else to go. Washing hung from the
15 windows. A goat stood on a corrugated roof, bleating at the water as if it could
   argue it away. On the steps that led down into the flood, a line of women were
   rinsing pots in the same water that had swallowed their homes.

   My job was to find the picture. I am not proud of how quickly I began to look for
   it. I moved from room to room, framing faces in my head before I had even said
20 hello: the old man with the bandaged foot, the baby asleep on a sack of rice, the
   boy staring out at the water with his chin on his knees. Each one, I calculated,
   might make a front page. I told myself this was how the world would be persuaded to
   care. Some of that was true. Some of it was simply habit.

   It was the boy who changed things. He could not have been more than ten. When he saw
25 my camera bag he stood up, wiped his hands carefully on his shorts, and offered to
   carry it. I said no, it was heavy. He insisted, quietly, the way a host insists that
   a guest takes the best chair. So I let him. He carried it from room to room behind
   me, holding it against his chest as if it were something precious, and every time I
   stopped he set it down gently on the driest patch of floor he could find.

30 At the end of the afternoon I asked the translator to find out his name. It was
   Issa. His family's house, he said, was the third roof from the left. He pointed it
   out as if he were showing me a fine view. Then he asked, very politely, whether my
   photographs would be in a newspaper, and whether the people who saw them would know
   that Kanta had a good school and a famous football team before the water came.

35 I did not know what to say. I had spent the whole day collecting pictures of what
   Kanta had lost. Not once had it occurred to me to ask what it had been. Issa did not
   want pity. He wanted the world to know that his village had been a place worth
   living in, and that it would be again. In his eyes, I realised, I was not a rescuer
   or a witness. I was a guest who had come at a bad time.

40 I took one more photograph before the boat left. It is not the picture that went on
   the front page; my editor preferred the baby on the rice sack. It shows Issa
   standing on the school steps with my camera bag still in his arms, grinning, the
   water behind him lit gold by the late sun. I keep it on my desk. When the figures
   start to pile up, as they always do, it reminds me that every one of them has a
45 name, a house that is the third roof from the left, and a football team that is
   famous, at least, in Kanta.

### english-language.q5.whiteout  (22 marks)
This question is based on both Text One, *Whiteout on the Ridge*, in the Insert, and Text Two, the extract from *127 Hours: Between a Rock and a Hard Place*, from Part 1 of the Anthology.
Text One: Whiteout on the Ridge
   It began, as these things often do, with a beautiful morning. When I left the car
   park at eight o'clock, the sky was a sharp, cloudless blue and the snow on the
   summit glittered like sugar. I had a map, a compass, spare gloves, a flask of soup
   and a forecast that promised a dry day with 'some cloud later'. I remember feeling
 5 slightly smug as I passed a group turning back at the first stile.

   By eleven I was on the ridge, a narrow spine of rock and old snow with steep drops
   on either side. The views were enormous. I could see three lakes, a scattering of
   farms and, far to the west, the grey glint of the sea. I stopped to eat a sandwich
   and take photographs, and it was only when I stood up again that I noticed the
10 cloud.

   It was coming up from the valley on my left, smooth and white, rolling over the edge
   of the ridge like milk boiling over a pan. I had perhaps five minutes before it
   reached me. I spent two of them deciding whether to go on or turn back, and by the
   time I had decided, the decision had been made for me. The world disappeared.

15 A whiteout is very hard to describe to anyone who has not been in one. It is not
   like fog. In fog you can see your own feet. In a whiteout the snow on the ground and
   the cloud in the air become exactly the same colour, and you lose all sense of up
   and down. Within minutes the wind had picked up, driving snow sideways into my face
   so hard that it stung like sand. I could not tell where the ridge ended and the drop
20 began.

   I did what I had been taught and stopped. I crouched down, got out my map and
   compass with fingers that had already gone stiff, and worked out a bearing that
   would take me off the ridge on its gentler, southern side. Then I began to walk,
   counting my paces out loud, stopping every hundred steps to check the needle. My
25 voice sounded thin and strange, as if it belonged to someone else.

   At one point the ground in front of me simply vanished. I stopped with one foot in
   the air, my heart hammering, and lowered myself onto my hands and knees. Ahead,
   where I had been about to step, was nothing but white. I threw a snowball forward
   and did not hear it land. I have no idea how deep that drop was. I have tried very
30 hard not to think about it.

   It took me three hours to come down a slope that would normally take forty minutes.
   When I finally stepped out of the bottom of the cloud, the change was so sudden that
   I almost laughed: green grass, a stone wall, a sheep staring at me with mild
   disapproval. I sat down on the wall and drank the whole flask of soup, which had
35 gone lukewarm, and which was the best thing I have ever tasted.

   I have walked in the mountains for twenty years, and I learned more that day than in
   all the others put together. I learned that a forecast is a guess, not a promise. I
   learned that the group who turned back at the stile were not timid but wise. Most of
   all I learned that the mountain does not care how experienced you are. It only cares
40 whether you are paying attention.

### english-language.q5.stammer  (22 marks)
This question is based on both Text One, *The Voice on the Radio*, in the Insert, and Text Two, the extract *Young and dyslexic? You've got it going on*, from Part 1 of the Anthology.
Text One: The Voice on the Radio
   Every weekday at six o'clock, about half a million people wake up to Danny Okoro's
   voice. It is warm, quick and full of jokes, the kind of voice that makes you feel
   the day might not be so bad after all. What most of his listeners do not know is
   that, until he was nineteen, Danny could not say his own name without getting stuck.

 5 'D was the worst,' he tells me, stirring three sugars into his coffee in a café near
   the studio. 'Which is unlucky, when your name is Danny. I used to introduce myself
   as Dan-Dan-Dan until people just started calling me that. Some of them meant it
   kindly. Most of them didn't.'

   School, he says, was a daily battle. He would count the pupils ahead of him when the
10 teacher took the register, rehearsing the word 'here' over and over in his head
   until his turn came, and then it would refuse to come out. He learned to swap
   difficult words for easier ones, so that his sentences became strange and
   roundabout. One teacher wrote on his report that he was 'bright but lazy in oral
   work'. Another made him read aloud in front of the class 'to build his confidence'.
15 He describes that lesson as the longest twenty minutes of his life.

   'I wasn't angry with them,' he says now. 'They didn't understand it. Nobody did
   then. They thought if I just tried harder, or relaxed, or breathed properly, it
   would go away. That's like telling someone with a broken leg to walk more
   confidently.'

20 The turning point came in an unlikely place: a hospital radio station, where his
   older cousin volunteered. Danny went along one evening to help carry boxes and ended
   up being handed a microphone when a presenter failed to turn up. 'I was terrified,'
   he says. 'But there was something about it. Nobody could see me. Nobody was looking
   at my face waiting for me to finish. It was just me and the music and these patients
25 lying in bed who were glad of a bit of company.' He stammered through the whole
   hour. A nurse rang in afterwards to say it was the best show they had had in months.

   He went back the next week, and the next. With the help of a speech therapist, he
   learned techniques for easing into difficult sounds, and, just as importantly, he
   learned to stop being ashamed of the moments when they did not work. He still
30 stammers occasionally on air. He does not edit it out. 'If I hide it, I'm telling
   every kid who stammers that it's something to hide,' he says. 'I'm not doing that.'

   Letters arrive at the studio every week, many of them from young listeners. One boy
   wrote to say he had answered the register for the first time after hearing Danny
   talk about it. Danny keeps that letter in his wallet. He takes it out to show me,
35 the paper soft from folding. 'That's worth more than any award,' he says.

   As we leave the café, a woman at the next table recognises his voice and asks for a
   photograph. Danny chats to her easily, and when he gets stuck for a moment on the
   word 'pleasure', he simply waits, smiles, and lets the word arrive. She does not
   seem to notice. Neither, I realise, do I.

### english-language.q5.sump  (22 marks)
This question is based on both Text One, *Below the Water Line*, in the Insert, and Text Two, the extract from *127 Hours: Between a Rock and a Hard Place*, from Part 1 of the Anthology.
Text One: Below the Water Line
   The entrance to Pwll Du is a crack in a limestone hillside, no wider than a wardrobe
   door, half hidden by ferns. I had been through it more than forty times. I knew the
   first chamber, the long crawl that followed it and the low, wet passage that the old
   guidebooks call the Letterbox. That morning the sky was a clean, washed blue and the
 5 forecast promised nothing worse than a light shower in the afternoon. I told my
   sister where I was going, as I always did, and said I would be back for tea.

   Caves are quiet in a way that the surface never is. Two hundred metres in, the only
   sounds were my own breathing, the scrape of my helmet on the roof and the steady
   drip of water finding its way down through the rock. My lamp threw a small circle of
10 yellow light ahead of me, and beyond it the darkness was total, thick and patient. I
   have always found that darkness comforting. It asks nothing of you except attention.

   I noticed the change in the stream first. On the way in it had been a polite trickle
   running beside the path. Now it was louder, and the colour had changed from clear to
   the brown of weak tea. I stopped and watched it for a full minute. It was rising.
15 Not quickly, but steadily, licking a little higher up the pebbles with every surge.
   Somewhere above me, the light shower had arrived early, and it had not been light.

   I turned back at once. Every caver knows the rule: when the water rises, you do not
   finish the trip, you do not take one more photograph, you go. The crawl took me
   twenty minutes on the way in. On the way out it took eight, and I do not remember
20 most of it. I remember the cold soaking through my suit at the knees and elbows. I
   remember the roar building behind me like an approaching train. And I remember
   reaching the Letterbox and seeing that the gap between the water and the roof, which
   had been the height of my forearm that morning, was now no wider than my hand.

   There is a particular kind of fear that arrives very calmly. I knelt in the water
25 and made myself think. Behind me, the passage was filling. Ahead, the Letterbox was
   perhaps four metres long. If I went through on my back, with my face turned up to
   the roof, I could keep my mouth in the air pocket for most of the way. If the pocket
   closed while I was halfway, I would be holding my breath in the dark with no way of
   turning round.

30 I took three slow breaths, the way I had been taught, and pushed myself in. The
   water closed over my ears and the roar became a dull, enormous hum. I could feel the
   rock pressing against the tip of my nose. I moved by inches, pulling with my
   fingertips on the ceiling, counting each pull out loud into the tiny space of air.
   Seven. Eight. Nine. At eleven my helmet jammed against something and for one long,
35 airless second I could not move at all.

   Then it came free, and the roof lifted, and I was lying in a pool on the far side,
   coughing and laughing at the same time. I do not know why I laughed. Perhaps it was
   the sheer surprise of still being alive. I crawled the last hundred metres to the
   entrance and came out into a grey, hammering downpour, the ferns bent flat and the
40 hillside streaming with water. It was the most beautiful weather I had ever seen.

   I went back to Pwll Du the following summer, in a long dry spell, with two friends
   and a great deal more respect. At the Letterbox I stopped and put my hand flat
   against the roof, where my fingertips had pulled me through. It felt like nothing at
   all: cool, damp, ordinary stone. The cave had not been trying to kill me. It had
45 simply been doing what water and rock have done for a million years, and I had
   happened to be in the way.
