### history.russia-investigation.0006  (24 marks)
Source A: From a letter written by a textile worker in St Petersburg to her brother in the countryside, 1907.
We sleep twelve to a room in the barracks next to the mill, and the air is so thick you can hardly breathe. The foreman fines us for talking and for every broken thread. Some of the men say we should strike again as we did in 1905, but I am afraid. They say the police have lists of all our names.
Source B: From the diary of a factory owner in Moscow, 1912.
The agitators are at work again. Since the shooting at the Lena goldfields, my men talk of nothing but strikes. Yet I pay better than most, and the government has now introduced insurance for sickness and accidents. The workers do not seem grateful. I shall ask the governor to send more police.
Extract C: From a study of Russian society, published in 2006.
It is easy to exaggerate the misery of Russian workers before the war. Industry was growing fast, and wages in some trades rose. The government introduced insurance schemes, and trade unions were legal, if closely watched. Most strikes were about pay, not politics. Had war not come in 1914, the regime might well have survived.

We arrived at the Tauride Palace on 5 January to find it surrounded by sailors with rifles. The galleries were full of armed men who jeered at our speeches. When we tried to return the next day, the doors were locked. The people had voted for us, and Lenin simply sent us home.
Source B: From a letter written by a peasant to a newspaper, December 1917.
At last the land is ours! The new government has given us the landlord's estate without payment. We have divided it fairly by the number of mouths in each family. Lenin never intended to share power. He closed opposition newspapers within days of taking office, set up the Cheka in December 1917 and dispersed the Constituent Assembly after a single day. By the summer of 1918 Russia was a one-party state ruled by terror.

### history.russia-investigation.0012  (24 marks)
Source A: From a speech by a socialist at a workers' meeting in St Petersburg, November 1905.
The Manifesto is a trick! The Tsar promises freedom with one hand and sends his Cossacks with the other. The liberals have run off to celebrate, but we will not be fooled. The Soviet must continue the struggle until the autocracy is destroyed.
Source B: From a government statement published in newspapers, October 1906.
His Majesty has granted his people the Duma and the freedoms of speech and assembly. Order has been restored throughout the empire. Those who used these freedoms to spread violence have been punished, and loyal subjects may now look forward to peace and progress.
Extract C: From a study of Russian politics, published in 2003.
The events of 1905 changed Russia more than is often admitted. For the first time, Russians could vote, form parties and trade unions, and read newspapers that criticised the government. The Duma, however weak, became a place where ministers were questioned in public. The autocracy would never be quite the same again.

We had brave men but no common aim. Some of our generals wanted the Tsar back; others wanted a republic.
