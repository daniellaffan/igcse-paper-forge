Life in thin air
The air at the top of a high mountain contains the same percentage of oxygen as
the air at sea level, about 21%. However, the air pressure is much
lower, so each breath contains fewer oxygen molecules. At 5 000 m above sea level,
a breath of air contains only about half as much oxygen as the same volume of air
at sea level.

Climbers who go up a mountain too quickly may suffer from altitude
sickness. They have headaches, feel sick and cannot sleep, and they find that even
gentle exercise makes them breathless. Their breathing rate and heart rate
increase, even when they are resting.

If a person stays at high altitude for several weeks, the body adjusts.
The kidneys release a hormone called EPO, which travels to the bone marrow
and stimulates it to make more red blood cells. In one study, the
concentration of red blood cells in the blood of eight climbers rose from a mean
of 5.0 million per mm³ at sea level to 6.1 million per mm³ after six weeks at 4
500 m.

People whose families have lived at high altitude for many generations
show other adaptations. Many people in the Himalayas have a wider chest and larger
lungs than people who live at sea level. They also breathe more rapidly and
their blood vessels widen more easily, so more blood flows to the tissues.
Scientists have found that many of these people carry a particular allele of a
gene involved in the response to low oxygen. This allele is rare in populations
that live at sea level.

Some athletes train at high altitude before a competition at sea level.
They believe that after training at altitude, their muscles will receive
more oxygen during a race and will build up less lactic acid. Others try to
increase their red blood cell count by injecting EPO. This is banned in sport,
partly because it makes the blood thicker and increases the risk of blood clots
and heart attacks.

Athletes have learned to make use of these changes. Many long-distance
runners spend several weeks training in mountain camps before an important race
held near sea level. While they are at altitude their bodies produce extra red
blood cells, and for a short time after they return these cells allow more oxygen
to be carried to their muscles. Some athletes sleep in special tents in which the
air contains less oxygen than normal, which produces a similar effect. Sports
organisations have debated whether such tents give an unfair advantage over other
competitors.

Bees, crops and insecticides
About three quarters of the world's food crops depend at least partly on animals
for pollination. Bees are the most important pollinators. As a bee feeds on
nectar, pollen grains stick to the hairs on its body and are carried to the next
flower it visits.

Nectar is a sugary liquid made by flowers. Bees use it as their main
source of energy, and they collect pollen as a source of protein for their young.
Flowers that are pollinated by insects often have large, brightly coloured
petals and a strong scent.

In many countries the number of bee colonies has fallen over the last
thirty years. Scientists think there are several causes, including loss of wild
flowers, a parasitic mite that spreads viruses between bees, and a group of
insecticides called neonicotinoids.

Neonicotinoids are taken up from the soil by crop plants and transported
to all parts of the plant, including the pollen and nectar. They kill
insect pests by binding to receptors at the synapses of nerve cells, so that nerve
impulses can no longer be controlled.

In one experiment, scientists set up 50 colonies of bumblebees. Twenty-five
colonies were given pollen and sugar solution containing a low concentration of a
neonicotinoid, similar to that found in crops. The other twenty-five colonies were
given pollen and sugar solution with no insecticide. After six weeks, the
colonies were placed in a field and allowed to feed normally. At the end of the
summer, the untreated colonies had produced a mean of 13.7 new queens each, but
the treated colonies produced a mean of only 2.0 new queens each.

Other studies have found that honeybees exposed to neonicotinoids are more
likely to get lost when returning to the hive.

Several countries have now restricted the use of neonicotinoids on flowering
crops. Some farmers are using biological control instead, for
example by releasing ladybirds to eat aphids, or by planting strips of wild
flowers at the edges of fields to provide food and shelter for predatory insects.

Ordinary people can also help pollinators. Planting a mixture of flowers
that open at different times of year gives bees a supply of nectar and pollen from
early spring to late autumn. Leaving areas of lawn uncut allows wild flowers such
as clover to bloom, and piles of dead wood provide nesting sites for solitary
bees. In one city, volunteers counted the insects visiting garden flowers each
summer for five years, and they recorded more species of bee in gardens where no
sprays were used than in gardens that were regularly treated.

Saving cassava
Cassava is a shrub grown for its large starchy roots. It is the main source
of carbohydrate for more than 500 million people in Africa, Asia and South
America. Cassava grows well in poor soils and survives long periods of
drought, so it is an important crop for small farmers.

Cassava brown streak disease is caused by a virus that is spread by
whiteflies, small insects that feed on the sap in the phloem of cassava leaves.
Infected plants show yellow patches on their leaves, and their roots
develop brown, dead areas that make them unfit to eat. In some regions the disease
destroys up to 70% of the crop.

Farmers usually grow new cassava plants from cuttings of stems taken
from the previous year's crop. If the parent plant was infected, the virus is
present in the cuttings as well.

Scientists have produced genetically modified cassava that is resistant to the
virus. They used a restriction enzyme to cut a short section of DNA from
the virus, and inserted it into a bacterial plasmid. The plasmid was used as a
vector to carry the DNA into cassava cells grown in a laboratory. When
the modified plants are infected, the inserted DNA allows the plant to destroy the
genetic material of the virus before it can multiply.

In a field trial, 200 modified plants and 200 unmodified plants were grown
in the same field for one year. At harvest, 4 of the modified plants and 152 of
the unmodified plants had damaged roots. The mean yield of roots was 22.5
tonnes per hectare for the modified plants and 9.0 tonnes per hectare for the
unmodified plants.

An alternative approach is selective breeding. Some cassava varieties
grown in East Africa show partial resistance to the disease. Breeders cross
these varieties with high-yielding varieties and select the best offspring over
several generations.

Some people are concerned about growing genetically modified crops. They
worry that genes may spread to wild relatives of the crop, or that the crop may
affect insects that feed on it. Others argue that the risk of famine is a far
greater danger than these possible effects.

Training farmers is an important part of the project. Many farmers save
stems from their own plants to start the next crop, so a plant that is infected
but shows only mild symptoms can pass the virus on without anyone noticing.
Advisers now visit villages to show farmers how to recognise yellow patches on the
leaves and how to destroy infected plants by burning them. Farmers who plant only
certified healthy stems have reported harvests that are almost twice as large as
those of neighbours who reuse stems from diseased fields.

When coral turns white
Coral reefs are built by tiny animals called coral polyps. Each polyp has a
soft body with a ring of tentacles around its mouth, which it uses to catch small
animals from the water, and it builds a hard skeleton of calcium carbonate around
itself. Over thousands of years these skeletons build up to form reefs that can be
hundreds of kilometres long.

Living inside the cells of the polyps are single-celled algae. The algae
photosynthesise and pass most of the glucose and amino acids they make to the
coral. In return, the coral provides the algae with carbon dioxide and
nitrogen-containing waste. The algae also give the coral its bright colours.

When the temperature of the sea water rises by just 1 °C above its normal
summer maximum for a few weeks, the algae stop working properly and are expelled
from the coral. Without the algae the coral turns white, which is called
bleaching. Bleached coral is not dead, but if the algae do not return within a few
weeks, the coral starves.

Scientists surveyed 100 reefs in the Pacific Ocean. Before a marine
heatwave, the mean cover of living coral was 42% of the sea floor. One year after
the heatwave, it had fallen to 17%. The number of fish species found on the
reefs also decreased, because many fish feed on coral or shelter in it.

The increase in sea temperature is linked to global warming. As well
as warming the oceans, carbon dioxide dissolves in sea water and makes it more
acidic. Corals find it harder to build their skeletons in more acidic water.

Some corals survive heatwaves better than others. Scientists have found
that the polyps that survived on one reef contained a different type of alga that
can tolerate higher temperatures. They are now growing young corals from
these survivors in tanks and transplanting them back onto damaged reefs.

Reefs that are not damaged by other human activities, such as sewage,
overfishing and sediment from building sites, recover more quickly after
bleaching.

Not all of the news is bad. Some corals on the same reef survive heatwaves
that kill their neighbours, and scientists are studying these individuals to find
out why. One idea is to grow young corals from the survivors in tanks and then
attach them to damaged parts of the reef. Another is to shade small, valuable
areas of reef during the hottest weeks of the year. Most researchers agree,
however, that these methods can only buy time, and that reefs will not be safe
unless the rise in sea temperature is slowed.

Bacteria against dengue
Dengue fever is caused by a virus that is passed to humans when they are bitten by
female Aedes mosquitoes. Infected people develop a high temperature, severe
headaches and pain in their joints, and in a small number of cases the disease
causes internal bleeding. There is no drug that kills the virus, so doctors can
only treat the symptoms.

Scientists have found a way to reduce the spread of dengue that does not rely on
insecticides. Wolbachia is a bacterium that lives inside the cells of many
insects but is not naturally found in Aedes mosquitoes. When Wolbachia is
introduced into the eggs of these mosquitoes, the adults that develop are much
less able to carry the dengue virus. The bacterium is passed from a
female mosquito to all of her offspring through the cytoplasm of her eggs.

In one trial, mosquitoes carrying Wolbachia were released once a week for
twelve weeks in half of the districts of a city. The other districts received no
mosquitoes. After two years, the number of people who needed hospital treatment
for dengue was recorded in both groups of districts. In the districts
where no mosquitoes were released there were 2 940 cases per 100 000 people,
compared with 680 cases per 100 000 people in the districts where Wolbachia
mosquitoes were released.

The scientists also took blood samples from patients who arrived at clinics with a
fever. The samples were tested for antibodies against the dengue virus.
Patients who turned out not to have dengue were used as a comparison group,
because they had visited the same clinics during the same months.

Some people were worried that releasing millions of mosquitoes would harm
other organisms. Mosquito larvae live in water and are eaten by fish and dragonfly
larvae, and adult mosquitoes are food for birds and bats. However, the released
mosquitoes belong to the same species that already lived in the city, so the size
of the population does not stay higher for long.

The method is cheaper than repeated spraying with insecticide, which has to
be carried out every few months. Spraying also has the problem that some
mosquitoes survive because they are resistant to the insecticide, and these
survivors produce the next generation. Wolbachia offers a long-lasting solution,
because once it is established in a population it remains there without further
releases.

Health workers expect dengue to become a problem in new places during the
coming decades. Aedes mosquitoes breed in small pools of still water, such as
rainwater trapped in old tyres, plant pots and blocked gutters, and warmer winters
allow the adults to survive further from the equator. A mosquito that carries a
pathogen from one person to another in this way is called a vector. Teams in
several countries are now rearing Wolbachia mosquitoes in large numbers, and they
are asking householders to report where the insects are seen so that the spread of
the bacterium can be mapped.

Walking away from diabetes
The concentration of glucose in the blood is normally kept between about 4 and 7
mmol per dm³. After a meal, the pancreas releases the hormone insulin,
which causes liver and muscle cells to take up glucose from the blood.
In the liver, the extra glucose is converted to glycogen and stored.

In type 2 diabetes, the pancreas still produces insulin, but the cells of
the body no longer respond to it properly. As a result, the blood glucose
concentration stays high for long periods. Over many years, high blood
glucose damages small blood vessels in the eyes, kidneys and feet, and increases
the risk of heart disease.

Worldwide, about 537 million adults had diabetes in 2021, and about 90%
of these had type 2 diabetes. The main risk factors are being overweight,
lack of physical activity and a diet high in sugar and fat. The risk also
increases with age and is higher in people with a family history of the disease.

In one study, 3 000 adults with high blood glucose were divided into two
groups. One group was given advice on diet and asked to walk briskly for 30
minutes a day, five days a week. The other group was given no advice.
After three years, 14% of the group given advice had developed type 2
diabetes, compared with 29% of the group given no advice.

Exercise helps because working muscles take up glucose from the blood even
without insulin. Regular exercise also increases the number of mitochondria
in muscle cells and the number of capillaries around them.

Before blood tests were available, doctors identified diabetes by testing
the urine for glucose. In a healthy person, all the glucose filtered into
the nephrons is reabsorbed back into the blood, but when the blood glucose
concentration is very high, some glucose remains in the urine.

People with type 2 diabetes are first treated with changes to their diet
and exercise. If this is not enough, they are given drugs that make their cells
more sensitive to insulin.

Because type 2 diabetes develops slowly, many people have the condition
for years before it is diagnosed. Some health services now offer a simple test to
adults over the age of forty, in which a small sample of blood is taken to measure
the glucose concentration. People whose glucose levels are higher than normal, but
not yet high enough to count as diabetes, are invited to join exercise and diet
programmes. Early results suggest that these programmes are cheaper to run than
treating the damage to eyes, kidneys and feet that uncontrolled diabetes can
cause.

Milk and the adult gut
Milk contains a sugar called lactose. To be absorbed, lactose must first
be broken down into two smaller sugars, glucose and galactose, by an enzyme called
lactase. Lactase is made by cells lining the small intestine. All healthy
babies produce lactase, because milk is their only food for the first months of
life.

In most mammals, including most humans, lactase production falls sharply
after a child stops feeding on milk. Adults who cannot make enough lactase are
described as lactose intolerant. When they drink milk, the lactose
passes undigested into the large intestine. There, bacteria use the lactose for
anaerobic respiration and produce gases and acids that cause bloating and pain.

In some human populations, however, most adults continue to make lactase
throughout their lives. This feature is called lactase persistence. It is caused
by a change in the DNA close to the lactase gene. The allele for lactase
persistence (L) is dominant to the allele for lactase non-persistence (l).

Lactase persistence is common in people whose ancestors kept cattle, goats or
camels for milk. For example, about 89% of adults in Denmark are lactase
persistent, compared with about 5% of adults in China. Scientists think
that when people first began to keep animals for milk, individuals who could
digest lactose as adults had an advantage. Milk is a good source of energy,
protein and calcium, and in times of food shortage these individuals were more
likely to survive and have children.

Lactose-intolerant people can often eat yoghurt and hard cheese without
any symptoms. During yoghurt making, Lactobacillus bacteria convert much of the
lactose into lactic acid. Lactase can also be bought as tablets, which
are swallowed just before a meal containing milk.

Doctors can test for lactose intolerance by giving a patient a drink
containing 50 g of lactose and measuring the concentration of glucose in the blood
every 30 minutes for two hours. In a person who makes lactase, the blood glucose
concentration rises by more than 1.1 mmol per dm³. In a lactose-intolerant person
it hardly rises at all.

People who cannot digest lactose do not always have to avoid dairy foods
altogether. In yoghurt and many hard cheeses, bacteria have already broken down
much of the lactose during manufacture, so these foods cause fewer symptoms. Some
supermarkets also sell milk to which the enzyme lactase has been added at the
factory. Doctors advise people who cut out dairy foods completely to find other
sources of calcium, such as green leafy vegetables, nuts and fish with soft bones,
because calcium is needed for healthy bones and teeth throughout life.

Meadows under the sea
Seagrasses are flowering plants that live completely under the sea in shallow
coastal water. They have roots that anchor them in sand and mud, and long
thin leaves that sway in the current. Unlike seaweeds, seagrasses produce
flowers, and their pollen is carried from one plant to another by moving water.

Seagrass meadows are among the most productive habitats on Earth. The
leaves absorb light and take in carbon dioxide dissolved in the sea water, and use
them to make glucose. Much of the carbon is stored in the roots and in the
mud beneath the meadow, where dead leaves and roots decay very slowly because the
mud contains little oxygen. Scientists estimate that one hectare of seagrass can
store up to 830 kg of carbon each year, compared with 230 kg for one hectare of
tropical rainforest.

The meadows also provide food and shelter for many animals. Young fish
hide among the leaves from predators, turtles and dugongs graze on the leaves, and
crabs and snails feed on the small algae that grow on the leaf surface.

However, seagrass meadows are disappearing at a rate of about 7% of their total
area each year. One major cause is fertiliser that is washed into the sea
from farmland after heavy rain. This leads to rapid growth of algae in the water
above the meadows. Other causes include damage from boat anchors and the
building of harbours.

In one restoration project, volunteers collected seeds from a healthy
meadow and scattered them over an area of bare sea floor. After four years
the area covered by seagrass had increased from 0 to 36 hectares, and the number
of fish species recorded at the site had increased from 8 to 30. To check
the success of the project, divers placed square frames with sides of 0.5 m at
random positions and counted the seagrass shoots inside each frame.

Protecting seagrass is now seen as one way to slow climate change. As
well as storing carbon, healthy meadows reduce the energy of waves and protect
coastlines from erosion.

To follow the recovery of the meadows, divers swim along fixed lines
across the sea bed every spring and record how much of the ground is covered by
leaves. They also cut small samples of leaf to measure how quickly new growth is
produced. Local fishing groups have agreed to keep boats out of the areas that are
being restored, because anchors and nets tear up the roots. Scientists hope that,
within twenty years, the replanted meadows will once again provide shelter for
young fish and food for turtles and sea birds.

A gene, a parasite and a map
Sickle-cell disease is an inherited condition that affects the haemoglobin in red
blood cells. It is caused by a change in a single base in the gene that
codes for one of the haemoglobin proteins. This change causes one amino acid
in the protein to be replaced by a different amino acid.

People with two copies of the sickle-cell allele make abnormal haemoglobin.
When the concentration of oxygen in their blood is low, the abnormal haemoglobin
molecules join together into long fibres, and the red blood cells become stiff and
curved like a sickle. Sickle cells can block capillaries, causing severe
pain, and they are destroyed more quickly than normal red blood cells, so the
person often feels tired and weak.

People with one normal allele (A) and one sickle-cell allele (S) are said
to be carriers. They make enough normal haemoglobin to be healthy, although they
may have mild symptoms when oxygen is very scarce.

Malaria is caused by a single-celled parasite called Plasmodium, which is
passed to humans by mosquitoes. The parasite spends part of its life cycle inside
red blood cells. Carriers of the sickle-cell allele are much less likely
to die from malaria than people with two normal alleles, because their infected
red blood cells are removed from the blood more quickly.

Maps show that the sickle-cell allele is most common in parts of Africa, the
Middle East and India where malaria is, or was, common. In some parts of
West Africa, up to 25% of the population are carriers, but in northern Europe the
allele is almost absent.

In many countries, newborn babies are now tested for sickle-cell disease
using a drop of blood taken from the heel. Couples who know that they are
both carriers can be given advice about the chance that their children will
inherit the disease.

Treatment includes drinking plenty of water, painkillers and
antibiotics to prevent infections. Some patients have been cured by a
transplant of bone marrow from a healthy donor. The bone marrow contains stem
cells that divide to produce all the types of blood cell.

Treatment for sickle cell disease has improved greatly. Children with the
condition are given extra fluids and pain relief during a crisis, and some take a
daily drug that increases the amount of a different form of haemoglobin in their
red blood cells. In a small number of patients, doctors have replaced the stem

A new antibiotic from the soil
Most of the antibiotics used by doctors were first discovered in microorganisms
that live in soil. Soil contains billions of bacteria and fungi in every
gram, and many of them release chemicals that kill or slow the growth of their
competitors. The first antibiotic to be widely used, penicillin, was
obtained from a mould called Penicillium.

Since the 1980s very few new types of antibiotic have been discovered,
while bacteria that are resistant to existing antibiotics have become more common.
In some hospitals, infections caused by resistant bacteria such as MRSA can
no longer be treated with the usual drugs.

One reason for the lack of new antibiotics is that about 99% of soil bacteria will
not grow on the nutrient agar used in laboratories. To solve this problem,
scientists designed a small plastic device with hundreds of tiny holes. A sample
of soil was diluted so that, on average, each hole received a single bacterial
cell. The device was then covered with a partially permeable membrane
and buried back in the soil. Nutrients from the soil could diffuse into the holes,
but the bacteria could not escape.

After two weeks, colonies had grown in many of the holes. The scientists
tested 10 000 of these colonies for the ability to kill Staphylococcus bacteria
and found 25 that did so. One of them produced a new antibiotic that kills
bacteria by stopping them making their cell walls.

The new antibiotic was tested on mice that had been infected with a dose of
Staphylococcus that is normally fatal. All twelve mice that were given the
antibiotic survived, while all twelve mice that were given an injection of salt
solution died within two days.

The scientists also grew Staphylococcus in a low concentration of the
new antibiotic for 27 days. No resistant bacteria appeared during this time.
They suggested that this is because the antibiotic attacks fatty molecules in
the cell wall rather than a protein, so a single mutation in a gene is unlikely to
change its target.

Before the antibiotic can be used to treat people, it must be tested for
safety in human volunteers. This process usually takes more than ten years.

Even if the new compound passes every stage of testing, it will be many
years before doctors can prescribe it. Each stage costs large sums of money, and
most compounds that look promising in the laboratory fail later because they are
found to damage human cells. The researchers are therefore collecting thousands
more soil samples from forests, deserts and river banks. They argue that the
microorganisms in a single handful of soil may hold the answer to infections that
cannot be treated today, provided that new drugs are used carefully once they are
found.

An old disease, a new vaccine
Tuberculosis (TB) is caused by a bacterium called Mycobacterium tuberculosis.
It is spread through the air when a person with the disease coughs or
sneezes, releasing tiny droplets that contain the bacteria. When the
droplets are breathed in, the bacteria settle in the alveoli of the lungs.

In most healthy people, phagocytes engulf the bacteria, but some bacteria
survive inside the phagocytes. These bacteria can remain inactive for many
years without causing symptoms. If the immune system becomes weak, for example
because of malnutrition or another infection, the bacteria start to multiply.

The symptoms of active TB include a cough that lasts for more than three
weeks, coughing up blood, fever, night sweats and loss of body mass. The
bacteria destroy the walls of the alveoli and cause scar tissue to form in the
lungs, so the patient becomes breathless.

In 2022, about 10.6 million people became ill with TB, and 1.3 million
died from the disease. Most of these cases were in Asia and Africa.

TB can be cured with a combination of four antibiotics taken every day for
six months. Patients often feel better after a few weeks, and some stop
taking their medicine before the end of the course. This has led to strains
of TB that are resistant to the two most effective antibiotics.

The BCG vaccine contains a weakened strain of a related bacterium that causes
TB in cattle. It is given to babies in countries where TB is common and
protects young children well, but it is less effective in adults.

Scientists are testing new vaccines. In one trial, 3 500 adults who had
inactive TB bacteria were given either the new vaccine or an injection of salt
solution. Neither the volunteers nor the doctors knew which injection each
person had received. After three years, 13 people in the vaccine group and 26
people in the salt solution group had developed active TB.

If the new vaccine is approved, the next challenge will be to make and
deliver enough doses for the countries that need it most. Many of the people at
greatest risk live far from hospitals, so health workers will need to travel to
villages with supplies kept cold in refrigerated boxes. Researchers also want to
find out how long the protection lasts, and whether a second dose is needed
several years later. They will continue to follow the volunteers from the trial
for at least ten years.

Farming upwards
By 2050 the world's population is expected to reach almost 10 billion, and much of
the extra food will be needed in cities. One possible solution is
vertical farming, in which crops are grown indoors on shelves stacked one above
another, sometimes in buildings that are twenty storeys high.

Most vertical farms do not use soil. Instead, the roots of the plants hang
in a solution of water containing all the mineral ions the plants need, including
nitrate and magnesium. Air is bubbled through the solution all the time.
The solution is pumped around the farm and reused, so a vertical farm
uses up to 95% less water than a field growing the same crop.

The plants are lit by light-emitting diodes (LEDs) that produce mainly red
and blue light. Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light strongly, but
reflects most green light. LEDs also produce much less heat than the lamps used in
older glasshouses, so they can be placed close to the leaves.

Inside the building, the temperature is kept at 24 °C and the carbon
dioxide concentration is raised to about three times the concentration in normal
air. Computers control the lights so that the plants receive 18 hours of
light each day.

Lettuce grown in one vertical farm reached harvest size in 30 days,
compared with 60 days in a field. The farm produced 105 kg of lettuce per
square metre of floor area each year, compared with about 3.5 kg per square metre
per year in a field.

Because the farm is sealed, there are very few insect pests, and no
pesticides are needed. If pests do get in, they are controlled by
releasing predatory insects.

The biggest disadvantage of vertical farming is the cost of the
electricity used to power the lights, pumps and air conditioning. If the
electricity comes from burning fossil fuels, the farm may contribute to climate
change. At present, vertical farming is only profitable for fast-growing,
high-value crops such as salad leaves and herbs. Staple crops such as wheat and
rice still need to be grown in fields.

Supporters of vertical farming argue that costs will fall as the
technology improves. LEDs have become far more efficient over the last twenty
years, and some farms now buy their electricity from solar and wind power.
Building farms close to the shops that sell the produce means that vegetables
travel only a few kilometres, so they are fresher and less fuel is used for
transport. Critics reply that a field uses free light from the Sun, and that it
will be difficult for a building full of lamps ever to compete on price.
